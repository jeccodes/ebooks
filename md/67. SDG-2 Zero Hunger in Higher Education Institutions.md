**Abstract**

Sustainable Development Goal 2 (SDG-2), Zero Hunger, is one of the most urgent global challenges that directly impacts human development, health, and socio-economic stability. It seeks to end hunger, achieve food security, improve nutrition, and promote sustainable agriculture by 2030. Despite global food production being adequate to feed the world, hunger and malnutrition persist due to poverty, inequality, climate change, conflict, and inefficient food distribution systems. Within this context, Higher Education Institutions (HEIs) play a pivotal role as knowledge generators, innovators, and community partners in advancing SDG-2. This book emphasizes how HEIs can integrate research, innovation, policy advocacy, and community engagement to address the multifaceted dimensions of hunger. It highlights the strategic importance of research on climate-smart agriculture, biofortified crops, post-harvest management, and food waste reduction, alongside the significance of awareness programmes on nutrition and sustainable consumption. By aligning with accreditation and ranking frameworks such as NAAC, NIRF, NBA, and QS I·GAUGE, HEIs can systematically document and showcase their contributions to SDG-2 while ensuring accountability and measurable outcomes. The book also underscores the role of campus initiatives such as zero food waste campaigns, student-led food redistribution drives, and the establishment of kitchen gardens and farmers’ markets, which create direct community impact. Furthermore, it stresses the need for capacity building among farmers through training programmes, technology adoption, and value addition initiatives, thereby improving productivity, incomes, and resilience. National and international collaborations with UN agencies, global universities, and agricultural research centres are presented as vital pathways for knowledge exchange, joint research, and policy development. MOOCs and digital platforms are discussed as fast-track strategies for disseminating knowledge on sustainable food systems to diverse stakeholders. The book situates SDG-2 within the broader accreditation and quality assurance ecosystem, providing evidence-based frameworks for HEIs to align their teaching, research, and extension activities with global sustainability benchmarks. It demonstrates how achieving SDG-2 not only strengthens food security and nutrition but also enhances institutional visibility, global rankings, and social responsibility. Ultimately, the book argues that hunger eradication is not merely a developmental priority but a moral imperative, and that HEIs, through their academic, research, and community functions, are uniquely positioned to act as catalysts of change. By integrating SDG-2 into their policies, curricula, and outreach, HEIs can contribute significantly to creating hunger-free, sustainable, and equitable societies while fulfilling their role as drivers of national development and global progress.

**1. SDG-2: End Hunger, Achieve Food Security and Improved Nutrition and Promote Sustainable Agriculture**

> • Core Goal
>
> o The United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goal 2 (SDG-2) is defined as “End hunger, achieve food security and improved nutrition, and promote sustainable agriculture by 2030.”
>
> o It emphasizes the elimination of hunger and malnutrition, particularly among vulnerable populations like children, women, and rural communities.
>
> • Context & Global Challenge
>
> o Despite global food production being sufficient to feed the world, millions still suffer from hunger and undernourishment due to unequal distribution, poverty, conflict, and climate change.
>
> o Food insecurity is compounded by climate-related risks, loss of biodiversity, soil degradation, and post-harvest losses.
>
> o Malnutrition persists in two forms: undernutrition (lack of sufficient calories or micronutrients) and overnutrition (rising obesity due to poor-quality diets).
>
> • Key Targets of SDG-2
>
> o End hunger and ensure access to safe, nutritious, and sufficient food for all people year-round.
>
> o End all forms of malnutrition, with a special focus on children under five, pregnant women, and the elderly.
>
> o Double agricultural productivity and incomes of small-scale food producers through access to land, resources, and technology.
>
> o Ensure sustainable food systems, including resilient agricultural practices that adapt to climate change and improve soil health.
>
> o Maintain the genetic diversity of seeds, crops, and livestock and promote fair access to resources.
>
> o Enhance investment in rural infrastructure, research, and agricultural extension services to support food production.
>
> • Role of HEIs in Achieving SDG-2
>
> o **Research & Innovation:** Conduct studies on food security, climate-smart agriculture, biotechnology, and nutritional sciences.
>
> o **Community Engagement:** Partner with farmers and rural communities for training, extension, and adoption of sustainable farming practices.
>
> o **Policy & Advocacy:** Provide evidence-based recommendations for government and NGOs on hunger alleviation strategies.
>
> o **Campus Initiatives:** Achieve zero food wastage through monitoring, redistribution of surplus food, and composting.
>
> o **Education & Awareness:** Run student-led initiatives, social campaigns, and food literacy programmes to promote responsible consumption.
>
> • Expected Global Impact
>
> o Ending hunger would improve human capital, productivity, and health outcomes.
>
> o Sustainable agriculture would ensure environmental conservation, economic resilience, and social equity.
>
> o Reducing food insecurity aligns with the human right to food and strengthens peace and stability worldwide.

**2. Key Recommendations to Achieve SDG-2: Zero Hunger**

> • Food Waste Monitoring and Zero-Waste Strategies in HEIs
>
> o Establish campus-level food waste audits to document wastage in hostels, canteens, mess facilities, and events.
>
> o Create digital dashboards or annual reports for transparency.
>
> o Develop strategies such as portion control, composting, redistribution through NGOs, and food banks to achieve zero food wastage.
>
> • R&D in Climate-Smart Agriculture and Biofortified Crops
>
> o Focus research on improving nutritional quality of food crops (e.g., iron-rich millet, vitamin A rice).
>
> o Develop and test climate-resilient technologies like precision irrigation, drought-resistant crops, and vertical farming.
>
> o Promote hi-tech and high-density farming to ensure continuous year-round agricultural production and stable cash flow for farmers.
>
> • Capacity Building of Farmers
>
> o Organize training and skill-development workshops on post-harvest management, cold storage, and processing of perishable crops.
>
> o Provide modules on branding, value addition, and market linkages to increase farmers’ income.
>
> o Use student extension projects and field internships to disseminate modern techniques at the grassroots.
>
> • Community Feeding and Incentivized Agricultural Practices
>
> o Involve religious trusts, NGOs, and philanthropic groups to run voluntary meal programs for vulnerable sections.
>
> o Provide policy and financial incentives for practices that enhance soil organic carbon (composting, organic farming, agroforestry).
>
> • Global and National Collaborations
>
> o Build partnerships with international universities, FAO, WFP, and global agricultural research centres for joint projects.
>
> o Promote academic exchanges, collaborative research, and student mobility in the domain of sustainable food systems, Agri-trade, and climate resilience.
>
> • Strengthening Agricultural Higher Education
>
> o Upgrade agricultural curricula to integrate sustainability, digital farming, and agribusiness skills.
>
> o Launch scholarships, skill hubs, and entrepreneurial incubation programs to attract youth into agriculture as a viable career path.
>
> o Professionally trained farmers with scientific and business knowledge will directly accelerate SDG-2 targets.
>
> • Optimizing Indian Agriculture through Research Studies
>
> o HEIs should prioritize resource-optimization research – e.g., crop intensification with less water, effective pest control, minimal chemical input.
>
> o Innovate affordable storage and transportation technologies (solar cold storage, warehouse digitization) to minimize post-harvest losses.
>
> • Government Initiatives in Food Storage & Transport
>
> o Improve public storage facilities with modern infrastructure (cold chains, silos, sealed warehouses).
>
> o Strengthen transport logistics with smart supply-chain monitoring and rural infrastructure upgrades.
>
> • Campus-Level Food Security Initiatives
>
> o Ensure nutritious, sustainable, and affordable food options for students and staff.
>
> o Conduct continuous “Eat Right” campaigns to promote healthy eating and reduce processed food consumption.
>
> o Promote on-campus food production through kitchen gardens, hydroponics, or student-led urban farming projects.
>
> o Host fresh food markets on campus to link local farmers directly with students and staff.
>
> o Establish awareness and monitoring mechanisms to reduce campus food waste (smart dustbins, awareness drives, reward systems).

**4. Context and current status of SDG-2 (Zero Hunger)**

> • Hunger and Malnutrition as Developmental Barriers
>
> o Hunger and malnutrition directly affect all aspects of human development—physical growth, cognitive abilities, productivity, and overall well-being.
>
> o The saying *“bhookhe bhajan na hoye Gopala”* underlines that no meaningful activity, spiritual or developmental, can be pursued on an empty stomach. Food security is therefore the foundation of human progress.
>
> • Global Status (FAO, 2021 data)
>
> o Around 2.37 billion people worldwide are unable to consume a balanced diet on a regular basis, highlighting the massive scale of food insecurity.
>
> o Approximately 810 million people remain undernourished globally, representing severe deficits in calorie and nutrient intake.
>
> o An alarming 22% of children under the age of five are stunted due to chronic malnutrition. Stunting is not only a health issue but also a major determinant of reduced cognitive development, poor educational outcomes, and diminished lifelong productivity.
>
> • Impact of COVID-19 Pandemic
>
> o The pandemic has disrupted global food supply chains, increased poverty levels, and worsened inequalities in access to food.
>
> o Lockdowns and economic slowdowns have hit vulnerable populations hardest, particularly daily wage earners and rural communities.
>
> o School closures affected mid-day meal and feeding programs, further exacerbating child malnutrition.
>
> • Challenges to Achieving SDG-2 by 2030
>
> o The goal of eradicating hunger and malnutrition is ambitious yet urgent, given current global trends.
>
> o Persistent issues include poverty, inequality in access to food, climate change impacts on agriculture, post-harvest losses, and weak food distribution systems.
>
> o Social and political conflicts, along with natural disasters, have displaced communities, pushing millions into food insecurity.
>
> • Key Takeaway
>
> o Hunger and malnutrition are not isolated issues but are deeply interlinked with poverty, health, education, and sustainability.
>
> o The urgency to address SDG-2 has heightened in the post-pandemic era, demanding coordinated efforts from governments, HEIs, international organizations, NGOs, and communities.

**5. SDG-2 Targets and Indicators**

Sustainable Development Goal 2 (SDG-2): Zero Hunger is one of the most ambitious global commitments, aimed at ending hunger, achieving food security, improving nutrition, and promoting sustainable agriculture by 2030. The United Nations has outlined a set of specific targets and measurable indicators to track progress and ensure accountability. These targets are interconnected, addressing both the immediate challenge of hunger and the structural transformations needed in food production systems.

> • Target 2.1: End Hunger and Ensure Access to Safe, Nutritious Food by 2030
>
> This target emphasizes universal access to adequate food throughout the year, with a special focus on vulnerable groups such as the poor, children, and marginalized communities.
>
> o ***Indicator 2.1.1: Prevalence of Undernourishment*** measures the percentage of people whose dietary intake falls below the minimum energy requirements.
>
> o ***Indicator 2.1.2: Prevalence of Food Insecurity*** assesses the share of people who lack regular access to sufficient and nutritious food, monitored through household surveys and food security scales.
>
> • Target 2.2: End All Forms of Malnutrition by 2030
>
> Beyond hunger, malnutrition in its multiple dimensions—stunting, wasting, obesity, and micronutrient deficiencies—remains a critical challenge. Special emphasis is placed on children under five, adolescent girls, pregnant and lactating women, and older persons.
>
> o ***Indicator 2.2.1: Prevalence of Childhood Stunting*** measures impaired growth and development due to chronic malnutrition.
>
> o ***Indicator 2.2.2: Prevalence of Childhood Malnutrition (Wasting and Overweight)*** captures acute malnutrition and obesity trends in children under five, providing a dual perspective on under- and over-nutrition.
>
> • Target 2.3: Double Agricultural Productivity and Incomes of Small-Scale Producers by 2030
>
> Smallholder farmers, pastoralists, and fishers form the backbone of food systems, yet they remain among the poorest groups. This target promotes equity in access to land, resources, markets, and knowledge.
>
> o ***Indicator 2.3.1: Production per Labour Unit*** tracks efficiency gains in agriculture.
>
> o ***Indicator 2.3.2: Income of Small-Scale Food Producers*** monitors improvements in livelihoods and resilience among vulnerable producer groups.
>
> • Target 2.4: Ensure Sustainable Food Production Systems by 2030
>
> Climate change, extreme weather, and resource degradation threaten global food security. This target pushes for climate-resilient agriculture and soil quality enhancement.
>
> o ***Indicator 2.4.1: Proportion of Agricultural Area Under Productive and Sustainable Agriculture*** measures the scale of sustainable practices adopted, linking food security to ecosystem preservation.
>
> • Target 2.5: Maintain Genetic Diversity in Seeds, Plants, and Animals by 2020
>
> Genetic diversity is the foundation of resilient agriculture. Conserving seeds, breeds, and wild relatives ensures adaptability to future challenges like pests, diseases, and climate stress.
>

**7. Global Concerns and Partnerships for SDGs**

> • Need for Partnerships
>
> o The global community recognizes that the success of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) depends on strong collaboration across different levels of society.
>
> o No single government or institution can address issues like poverty, hunger, climate change, and inequality alone; these challenges require multistakeholder cooperation.
>
> • Emergence of Networks
>
> o To accelerate SDG implementation, new networks and alliances are being formed between:
>
> o National governments (policy design, funding, implementation).
>
> o Local governments (grassroots adoption, community-based programs).
>
> o NGOs/INGOs (capacity-building, advocacy, and outreach).
>
> o Universities (research, innovation, education, and evidence generation).
>
> o Policy forums (knowledge exchange and best practice dissemination).
>
> • Example: UNESCO–University of Bergen Partnership
>
> o UNESCO has partnered with the University of Bergen (Norway) to establish the Global Independent Expert Group on the Universities for the 2030 Agenda (EGU-2030).
>
> o Supporting partners include:
>
> o International Science Council (ISC)
>
> o International Association of Universities (IAU)
>
> • Mandate of EGU-2030
>
> o To develop guidelines and frameworks for how universities can actively contribute to the achievement of SDGs.
>
> o Key areas of focus include:
>
> o Knowledge development – generating relevant research on sustainability challenges.
>
> o Research strategies – aligning academic research with the SDG targets.
>
> o Education strategies – embedding sustainability into curricula, pedagogy, and student engagement.
>
> • Significance
>
> o Such collaborations highlight the central role of universities as both knowledge hubs and social actors.
>
> o They emphasize the triple role of HEIs:
>
> o Educators of socially responsible and sustainability-minded graduates.
>
> o Researchers generating innovations for sustainable development.
>
> o Community partners implementing outreach programs to achieve local SDG targets.
>
> o This model strengthens the global-local nexus, ensuring that universities contribute to both international frameworks and community-level impact.

>

>
> o Collaborate with ICAR (Indian Council of Agricultural Research) and global universities to design multidisciplinary MOOCs.
>
> o Use MOOCs for capacity building, enabling students, farmers, and policymakers to access training anytime, anywhere.

**Significance**

> • MOOCs can scale SDG education rapidly without requiring extensive physical infrastructure.
>
> • By supplementing traditional research and teaching, MOOCs can build awareness, technical knowledge, and problem-solving skills among large populations.
>
> • In the Indian context, MOOCs can serve as a bridge between farm universities, government schemes, and grassroots communities, accelerating the achievement of SDG-2 and related SDGs.

**10. Strategic Focus Areas for Achieving SDG-2**

> • Making Agriculture Productive, Attractive, and Sustainable
>
> o Agriculture must be repositioned as a knowledge-intensive and innovation-driven profession rather than a low-income livelihood option.
>
> o Adoption of scientific, innovative, and climate-smart technologies (e.g., precision farming, smart irrigation, biotechnologies, IoT-based monitoring) can increase productivity while reducing environmental stress.
>
> o To make farming attractive, it must be professionalized by:
>
> o Creating academic pathways in agricultural entrepreneurship.
>
> o Encouraging investment in agritech startups.
>
> o Building ecosystems that attract talent (youth, scientists) and capital (investors, venture funding) into the sector.
>
> o This transformation supports SDG-2 targets on doubling productivity and incomes of farmers while ensuring long-term sustainability.
>
> • Creating a Conducive Policy Climate through Advocacy Hubs
>
> o HEIs should act as policy think tanks by setting up Advocacy Hubs dedicated to SDG-2 targets (e.g., hunger eradication, malnutrition reduction, climate-resilient farming).
>
> o These hubs can:
>
> o Conduct research-based policy analysis and disseminate best practices.
>
> o Build platforms for dialogue between government, industry, NGOs, and academia.
>
> o Track progress towards food security targets and advise policymakers.
>
> o Such hubs help create a favorable policy ecosystem where innovation, investments, and sustainable farming practices can thrive.
>
> • Active Association with UN Agencies and International Networks
>
> o Universities should strengthen collaborations with global UN agencies and international sustainability associations to share knowledge and enhance the effectiveness of SDG-2 initiatives.
>
> o Examples include:
>
> o **ACTS (Australasian Campuses Towards Sustainability)** – Promotes sustainability practices across higher education institutions.
>
> o **EAUC (Environmental Association for Universities and Colleges, UK)** – Provides a platform for universities to address climate and sustainability goals.
>
> o **AASHE (Association for the Advancement of Sustainability in Higher Education, USA)** – Offers resources, benchmarking, and networks for advancing sustainability.
>
> o Active participation in such associations allows HEIs to:
>
> o Benchmark their performance against global best practices.
>
> o Gain access to international resources, case studies, and technical expertise.
>
> o Build regional and global coalitions for addressing hunger and food security in a more integrated manner.

**Significance of These Recommendations**

> • By making farming climate-smart and professional, HEIs can contribute to food security, sustainable livelihoods, and youth engagement in agriculture.
>
> • Advocacy hubs will bridge the gap between research and policy, ensuring that decisions are evidence-based and aligned with SDG-2 targets.
>
> • International partnerships ensure global-local synergy, so HEIs in India and elsewhere are not working in isolation but are part of a larger coordinated framework.

**14. Minimal Recommended Actions for HEIs under SDG-2 (Target 2.1: End Hunger & Ensure Access to Nutritious Food)**

> • Student Volunteering for Food Security
>
> o Encourage student-led groups (NSS, NCC, social clubs) to participate in food security initiatives.
>
> o Activities include collecting surplus food from hostels, canteens, hotels, and events, ensuring it is hygienically stored and redistributed.
>
> o Collaborations with local municipalities, NGOs, and other HEIs can expand the scale of such activities.
>
> o **Impact:** Reduced food wastage, immediate relief to vulnerable communities, increased student social responsibility.
>
> • Exchange Relationships with Regional Universities
>
> o Develop student and faculty exchange programmes focused on food security, nutrition awareness, and hunger alleviation.
>
> o Conduct joint field-level awareness programmes about government schemes like the Public Distribution System (PDS) and Midday Meal Scheme (MDM).
>
> o **Impact:** Improved knowledge dissemination, stronger community engagement, enhanced inter-university collaboration.
>
> • Greenhouse Promotion and Training
>
> o Organize training programmes for farmers and rural communities on greenhouse cultivation.
>
> o Promote adoption of protected cultivation technologies to ensure year-round availability of nutritious food.
>
> o Facilitate low-cost greenhouse designs tailored to local conditions.
>
> o **Impact:** Increased farmer incomes, availability of off-season crops, and greater food security resilience.
>
> • Continuous R&D for Food Quality and Shelf-life Improvement
>
> o Focus research on quality improvements in food crops (nutritional content, climate resilience).
>
> o Invest in studies on extending shelf-life of perishables through processing innovations and packaging technologies.
>
> o Collaborate with food industry for pilot testing and scaling solutions.
>
> o **Impact:** Reduction in post-harvest losses, enhanced nutritional outcomes, stronger food processing industry linkages.
>
> • Food Waste Reduction on Campus and in Communities
>
> o Introduce Save Food Campaigns within HEIs to educate students and staff about mindful consumption.
>
> o Establish campus food waste monitoring systems and redistribute excess food to community kitchens.
>
> o Conduct awareness drives to address socio-cultural factors behind wastage (e.g., taboos around reusing or sharing food at gatherings).
>
> o **Impact:** Behavioral change towards food waste, improved cultural awareness, reduced local hunger.
>
> • Collaborative Research to Reduce Post-Harvest Losses
>
> o HEIs, especially agricultural institutions, should focus on R&D to minimize post-harvest losses, currently estimated at 30–40% in India.
>
> o Research on primary processing technologies, cold chain logistics, and supply chain management can transform food availability.
>
> o Encourage student dissertations and funded projects in this area.
>
> o **Impact:** Improved efficiency in agricultural markets, reduced food insecurity, better farmer profitability.
>
> • Curriculum Integration for Sustainable Food Systems
>
> o HEIs, particularly agricultural universities, should integrate target-wise SDG-2 action plans into curricula.
>

**38. Research Area: Food Security**

SDG-2 Target: *2.1 – End hunger and ensure access by all people, in particular the poor and vulnerable, to safe, nutritious and sufficient food all year round.*

**Need of Research**

Food security is not only about producing food but also about ensuring its availability, accessibility, quality, and sustainability. Addressing hunger requires a holistic understanding of the technical (production techniques, technology use), environmental (climate-smart farming, land management), economic (supply chain, affordability), and social dimensions (equity, inclusivity, food justice). Without integrating these dimensions, interventions risk being unsustainable or inequitable.

**Key Activities / Actions**

1\. Sensitization against food wastage

> • **Action:** Form student and faculty groups in HEIs to run “Save Food” campaigns, monitor food waste in hostels, canteens, and events, and redistribute surplus food hygienically to needy communities.
>
> • **Impact:** Reduces wastage, fosters social responsibility, and directly aids vulnerable populations.

2\. Modernization of hostel kitchens

> • **Action:** Upgrade institutional kitchens with energy-efficient stoves, biogas plants, waste-to-energy systems, and cold storage facilities to improve hygiene and sustainability.
>
> • **Impact:** Reduces energy and food loss, while creating demonstration models for wider adoption.

3\. Protected cultivation research

> • **Action:** Encourage farmers to adopt polyhouses, greenhouses, and vertical farming for year-round production. HEIs can research designs, materials, and structures that withstand local climatic stresses like wind pressure, heat, and floods.
>
> • **Impact:** Ensures stable supply, protects farmers from climate shocks, and supports income stability.

4\. Post-harvest loss reduction

> • **Action:** Conduct R&D on storage methods, packaging innovations, cold chain logistics, and natural preservatives to extend shelf-life of perishable crops (e.g., fruits, vegetables, dairy).
>
> • **Impact:** Reduces the current 30–40% post-harvest losses, ensuring more food reaches consumers.

5\. Government-supported food storage & processing facilities

> • **Action:** Advocate for public-private partnerships to build modern warehouses, cold storages, food processing parks, and logistics hubs.
>
> • **Impact:** Increases efficiency, reduces spoilage, and makes food available year-round.

6\. Private sector and philanthropic involvement

> • **Action:** Encourage NGOs, religious institutions, and corporate CSR bodies to run food banks, community kitchens, and farmer-producer companies. Incentivize private investment in supply chain management.
>
> • **Impact:** Expands outreach, reduces dependency on government alone, and strengthens community support systems.

7\. Monitoring food distribution schemes

> • **Action:** HEIs can audit and evaluate central and state schemes like PDS, Mid-Day Meal, and Anganwadi services to ensure food reaches the intended beneficiaries, with quality maintained.
>
> • **Impact:** Enhances transparency, reduces corruption, and improves nutritional outcomes.

8\. Land-use planning

> • **Action:** Policy and research inputs to restrict large-scale diversion of fertile agricultural lands to industrial or urban purposes. Encourage zoning laws and land-use audits.
>
> • **Impact:** Safeguards long-term food security by preserving arable land.

**Consolidated Insights**

> • HEIs are positioned as knowledge generators and facilitators – leading research, training, and community outreach.
>
> • Government agencies provide policy, infrastructure, and incentives.
>
> • Private sector and NGOs bring in funding, efficiency, and last-mile delivery.
>
> • Collectively, these actions tackle SDG-2 from production, preservation, distribution, and consumption angles.

**46. SDG-2.5: Crop Gene Bank Preservation**

**1. Background and Rationale**

Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 2 aims for Zero Hunger, and within it, Target 2.5 focuses on safeguarding genetic diversity of seeds, cultivated plants, and farmed and domesticated animals and their related wild species.

Why is this critical?

> • **Erosion of genetic diversity:** With industrial farming, farmers are abandoning traditional varieties, leading to genetic uniformity. This makes crops vulnerable to pests, diseases, and climate extremes.
>
> • **Climate change impacts:** Droughts, floods, and heatwaves threaten food production. Conserving climate-resilient landraces and wild relatives ensures a backup pool for breeding adaptive crops.
>
> • **Nutrition security:** Many indigenous and underutilized crops are rich in micronutrients (iron, zinc, vitamin A). Losing them means losing natural nutrition solutions.

**2. What is Crop Gene Bank Preservation?**

It refers to systematic conservation, cataloguing, and storage of seeds, plant varieties, and animal genetic resources for future use in breeding, research, and sustainable farming.

Forms include:

> • **Ex-situ conservation:** Seed banks, cryo-preservation, tissue culture repositories.
>
> • **In-situ conservation:** On-farm conservation of landraces, community seed banks, wild habitats.
>
> • **Digital preservation:** Genetic sequencing databases and biodiversity registries.

Global examples:

> • Svalbard Global Seed Vault (Norway) – Backup for world's seeds.
>
> • ICAR-NBPGR (India) – National Bureau of Plant Genetic Resources holds \>4 lakh seed accessions.

**3. HEI Role in SDG-2.5**

HEIs, especially agricultural, biotechnology, and life sciences institutions, are uniquely positioned to lead gene bank initiatives:

1\. Survey & Documentation

> • Conduct field surveys to identify and catalog traditional crop varieties and indigenous breeds.
>
> • Use GIS and AI-based mapping for biodiversity hotspots.

2\. Establishing Gene Banks

> • Set up seed vaults, cryogenic labs, and tissue culture repositories.
>
> • Develop local community seed banks in partnership with farmers.

3\. Research & Development

> • Work on superior germplasm selection for yield, nutrition, and stress resistance.
>
> • Apply molecular breeding, CRISPR, and biotechnological tools to improve diversity.

4\. Conservation of Wild Relatives

> • Study and conserve wild species related to staple crops (e.g., wild rice, millet, sorghum).
>
> • Integrate them into crop improvement programs.

5\. Branding & Intellectual Property (IPR)

> • File for Geographical Indications (GI) and patents for indigenous varieties.
>
> • Work with Farmer Producer Organizations (FPOs) for branding local crops.

6\. Capacity Building

> • Train farmers in quality seed production and seed conservation.
>
> • Introduce curriculum modules on agrobiodiversity and SDG-2.

**4. Expected Outcomes**

By implementing SDG-2.5 actions, HEIs can contribute to:

> • **Food security & resilience –** Availability of climate-resilient, disease-resistant crops.

**5. Why Critical**

**50. SDG-2.1: End Hunger and Ensure Access to Nutritious Food**

**Minimal Recommended Actions for Government / Policymakers**

> • **Explanation:** Accurate, real-time data on hunger is often missing or outdated. Without it, interventions are misdirected or inadequate. A national/state-level Hunger Atlas or Dashboard can help in identifying the exact families, regions, and demographics that need urgent interventions.
>
> • **Policy Action:** Regular surveys (district-level) on hunger, malnutrition, and food insecurity; integrate data with Aadhaar-linked welfare schemes for accurate targeting.
>
> • **Expected Impact:** Ensures targeted distribution of food grains, minimizes leakages, and improves the effectiveness of government nutrition schemes.

2\. Introduce Measures to Reduce Food Waste

> • **Explanation:** A large portion of food is lost due to unscientific harvesting methods, poor storage, and inadequate cold chain facilities. For perishables (fruits, vegetables, dairy, meat), post-harvest wastage is 30–40%.
>
> • **Policy Action:**
>
> o Strengthen cold chain logistics, rural packhouses, and warehousing.
>
> o Enforce regulations on safe food handling, packaging, and scientific harvesting.
>
> o Create food redistribution policies (like linking hotels/restaurants with food banks).
>
> • **Expected Impact:** Drastically reduces food losses, increases availability of affordable food, and improves farmers' income.

3\. Incentivize Private Sector Investments in Food Processing and Supply Chains

> • **Explanation:** Public resources alone cannot manage the scale of food security challenges. The private sector (agribusinesses, FPOs, logistics firms) can modernize food processing, storage, and supply chains.
>
> • **Policy Action:**
>
> o Tax breaks, subsidies, and credit support for companies investing in cold storage and food processing.
>
> o Encourage PPP (Public-Private Partnership) in agri-logistics.
>
> • **Expected Impact:** Improves efficiency, creates jobs, enhances food affordability, and reduces farm-to-market wastage.

4\. Encourage Farmers to Adopt Protected Cultivation

> • **Explanation:** Climate change and erratic rainfall threaten food production. Protected cultivation (polyhouses, greenhouses, hydroponics, aquaponics) ensures year-round production and minimizes weather risk.
>
> • **Policy Action:**
>
> o Subsidize protected farming structures and materials.

**52. SDG-2.2: End Malnutrition and Ensure Sustainable Nutrition**

The SDG-2.2 target is to end all forms of malnutrition by 2025, including stunting and wasting in children under 5 years of age, and address the nutritional needs of adolescent girls, pregnant and lactating women, and older persons. Achieving this requires systemic interventions by governments and policymakers at multiple levels — food production, distribution, awareness, and health services.

1\. Emphasize and incentivize production of biofortified crops

> • **Policy Role:** Governments can integrate biofortified varieties (rich in iron, zinc, vitamin A) into national agricultural policies and link them with schemes like the Public Distribution System (PDS), Integrated Child Development Scheme (ICDS), and mid-day meals.
>
> • **Impact:** Expands access to nutrient-dense food staples and directly combats micronutrient deficiencies (e.g., anemia, night blindness).
>
> • **Evidence:** Seed distribution records, ICAR project reports, and MoUs with agricultural universities.

2\. Increase allocation towards fortified mid-day meals and rations

> • **Policy Role:** Expand the POSHAN Abhiyaan and allocate more funds to mid-day meals, Anganwadi centers, and hospital nutrition programs. Introduce digital monitoring (POSHAN tracker apps).
>
> • **Impact:** Provides balanced nutrition to vulnerable groups (children and mothers), reducing stunting, wasting, and low birth weight.
>
> • **Evidence:** Budget allocation orders, annual POSHAN progress reports, nutrition outcome surveys.

3\. Conduct national awareness programs on malnutrition

> • **Policy Role:** Use campaigns at schools, Panchayats, and mass media to educate families about dietary diversity, fortified foods, and healthy practices.
>
> • **Impact:** Changes food behavior, empowers communities to make healthier choices, and reduces ignorance-led malnutrition.
>
> • **Evidence:** Campaign posters, training materials, event reports with photos, attendance registers.

4\. Increase area under high-yielding and short-duration varieties

> • **Policy Role:** Provide subsidies, crop insurance, and demonstration programs through Krishi Vigyan Kendras (KVKs).
>
> • **Impact:** Improves food grain availability, ensures food security despite climate shocks, and helps farmers get faster returns.
>
> • **Evidence:** State agricultural production reports, KVK demonstration data, farmer adoption records.

5\. Encourage raising high-yielding animal breeds

> • **Policy Role:** Animal husbandry departments should introduce breed improvement programs, veterinary services, and fodder subsidies.
>
> • **Impact:** Improves supply of milk, eggs, and meat, which enhances rural incomes and reduces protein malnutrition.
>
> • **Evidence:** Breed improvement program reports, livestock census data, veterinary service records.

6\. Strengthen Pradhan Mantri Kisan SAMPADA Yojana

> • **Policy Role:** Develop food parks, cold storage, and processing clusters under the Ministry of Food Processing Industries (MoFPI). Promote PPP investments.
>
> • **Impact:** Reduces food wastage, creates rural employment, increases exports, and raises farmer incomes.
>
> • **Evidence:** MoFPI progress reports, audited expenditure data, records of agro-processing clusters.

7\. Incentivize pulses production

> • **Policy Role:** Increase MSP (Minimum Support Price), provide input subsidies, and promote awareness about the role of pulses in food security.
>
> • **Impact:** Enhances protein security, supports nitrogen-fixing crops that improve soil fertility, and strengthens rural nutrition.
>
> • **Evidence:** MSP procurement records, NFSM (National Food Security Mission) reports, farmer beneficiary data.

**Why This Matters**

All these actions directly link to SDG-2.2 outcomes — reducing stunting, wasting, and micronutrient deficiencies. They also complement other SDG goals like:

> • **SDG-3 (Health & Well-being):** Better nutrition reduces disease burden.
>
> • **SDG-12 (Responsible Consumption):** Reducing food waste promotes sustainable consumption.
>
> • **SDG-13 (Climate Action):** Climate-smart agriculture ensures long-term food security.

**How HEIs Can Support**

While these are policy-level actions, HEIs (Higher Education Institutions) can:

> • Conduct impact analysis studies of these schemes.
>
> • Provide scientific research on biofortification, storage, and processing technologies.
>
> • Run awareness campaigns among students and local communities.
>
> • Partner with government for monitoring and evaluation.

**54. SDG-2.3 (Productivity, Incomes, Value-chains): Government/Policy Maker**

1\. Creation of a Comprehensive SDG-2 Database

> • **What it means:** A structured, evidence-based data system (similar to *Cost of Cultivation Schemes* or *Agricultural Economics Research Centres – AERCs*) is essential for informed policy-making.
>
> • **Government role:** Designate nodal Higher Education Institutions (HEIs) (such as Agricultural Universities) to develop and maintain this database. Government agencies like Ministry of Agriculture, state departments, and statistical organizations would share relevant data.
>
> • **Why it matters:** Without granular, real-time data on indicators like food production, post-harvest losses, prices, market access, and nutrition outcomes, planning remains reactive instead of proactive.
>
> • **Impact:** Provides policymakers with clarity on vulnerable areas, helps track SDG progress, and enables better allocation of resources.

2\. Strengthening the KVK Network and Promoting Agri-Clinics

> • **What it means:** Krishi Vigyan Kendras (KVKs) are frontline agricultural extension centers, but many are under-staffed or lack modern technology. Agri-clinics, run by private entrepreneurs, can complement these services.
>
> • **Government role:** Increase funding, upgrade KVK facilities, and incentivize private sector agri-clinics (via PPP, subsidies, or CSR schemes).
>
> • **Why it matters:** Farmers require access to timely advisory services, quality inputs, soil testing, farm mechanization, and post-harvest solutions. Strengthening these support services reduces inefficiencies and improves productivity.
>
> • **Impact:** Empowered farmers with knowledge, reduced crop failures, adoption of modern farming practices, and higher yields.

3\. Strengthening e-NAM and Perishables Supply Chains

> • **What it means:** The National Agriculture Market (e-NAM) is a digital platform to link farmers with buyers across India. However, smallholders still struggle to access it. Perishable commodities like fruits, vegetables, and milk need robust cold chains and logistics.
>
> • **Government role:** Simplify onboarding for small/marginal farmers and FPOs, subsidize logistics and cold-storage infrastructure, and enforce quality assurance/assaying systems for digital trading.
>
> • **Why it matters:** Farmers often suffer from market asymmetry (low prices at farm-gate vs. high prices in retail). Strengthening e-NAM and perishables logistics bridges this gap and reduces wastage (30–40% in perishables currently).
>
> • **Impact:** Higher price realization for farmers, reduced post-harvest losses, better consumer access to affordable fresh produce.

4\. Promoting Value Addition and Branding of Farm Produce

> • **What it means:** Instead of selling raw produce, farmers can earn more by processing, packaging, and branding (e.g., millet flour, organic honey, cold-pressed oils).
>
> • **Government role:** Provide capital subsidies, skill training, and infrastructure support for Farmer Producer Organizations (FPOs) to set up small-scale processing units. Encourage private sector investments in large-scale clusters and food parks.
>
> • **Why it matters:** Value addition increases shelf life, improves food safety, and enhances market competitiveness. Branding creates consumer trust and better price margins.
>
> • **Impact:** Farmers' income stability, rural job creation, and contribution to exports and food processing industries.

5\. Promoting Crop and Farm Enterprise Diversification

> • **What it means:** Moving beyond monocropping to include high-value horticulture, pulses, livestock, fisheries, and allied enterprises like mushroom or beekeeping.
>
> • **Government role:** Strengthen agricultural universities' research and extension systems to provide models for region-specific diversification. Provide insurance/risk support for new enterprises.
>
> • **Why it matters:** Diversification reduces dependency on one crop, provides multiple income streams, and improves nutritional security. It is also climate-resilient since diverse systems cope better with shocks.
>
> • **Impact:** Sustainable income growth, improved soil health, resilience against market and climate risks, and stronger rural economies.

**56. SDG-2.4 (Ensure sustainable food production systems and implement resilient agricultural practices)**

1\. Professionalizing Agriculture with Technology and AI

> • **What it means:** Agriculture must be seen as a skilled, professional sector rather than an informal livelihood. By integrating advanced technologies—such as AI-driven irrigation systems, precision farming, drones, and IoT sensors—farmers can optimize the use of water, fertilizer, and land resources.
>
> • **HEI Role:** Agricultural universities and engineering HEIs can research AI applications in crop monitoring, water-use efficiency, and pest detection. They can also provide training and incubate start-ups in agri-tech.
>
> • **Impact:** Makes agriculture attractive to youth, increases productivity, reduces costs, and ensures sustainability.

2\. Rainwater Harvesting and Watershed Development

> • **What it means:** Since Indian agriculture is rain-dependent, augmenting irrigation potential through rainwater harvesting structures (farm ponds, check dams, percolation tanks) is critical. Micro and macro watershed approaches can improve soil moisture and groundwater recharge.
>
> • **HEI Role:** Research on watershed management models, field-level demonstrations, and farmer training. Civil engineering HEIs can contribute with structural designs.
>
> • **Impact:** Increased water availability, reduced dependency on erratic monsoons, improved cropping intensity.

3\. Stabilizing Crop Production Niches

> • **What it means:** Crops like saffron, kalajeera, cardamom, and apple require very specific climatic and ecological niches. Climate change and development projects threaten these niches. Research is required to map, monitor, and stabilize these environments.
>
> • **HEI Role:** Long-term funded projects to study microclimates, soil health, and adaptive farming techniques; policy briefs to guide local planning.
>
> • **Impact:** Protects high-value niche crops, sustains rural economies, and safeguards biodiversity.

4\. Promoting Soil Organic Carbon (SOC)

> • **What it means:** Indian soils have low SOC, particularly in the top plough layer, leading to poor fertility and declining yields. Techniques like green manuring, vermicomposting, crop residue management, and bio-waste conversion can enhance SOC.
>
> • **HEI Role:** Research on effective organic soil practices, farmer education, and integrating SOC awareness into curricula.
>
> • **Impact:** Improved soil fertility, higher productivity, better carbon sequestration, and climate resilience.

5\. Consolidation of Landholdings

> • **What it means:** Fragmented small landholdings reduce productivity and efficiency in both production and marketing. Policy emphasis on cooperative farming, farmer-producer organizations (FPOs), and flexible land-lease systems can make farming economically viable.
>
> • **HEI Role:** Policy research on land pooling models, cooperative economics, and social awareness campaigns.
>
> • **Impact:** Improved economies of scale, mechanization, efficient marketing, and better farmer income.

6\. Strengthening Research and Extension in Sustainable Agriculture

> • **What it means:** Research on sustainable farming systems, conservation agriculture, organic farming, and climate-smart crops must be scaled up. Extension wings of the government and agri-HEIs (like Krishi Vigyan Kendras, outreach centers) must be strengthened to bridge productivity gaps between progressive and average farmers.
>
> • **HEI Role:** Develop region-specific technologies, conduct extension activities, and document best practices.
>
> • **Impact:** Improved productivity, technology diffusion, and reduced gap between best and average farmers.

7\. Preventing Diversion of Agricultural Land

> • **What it means:** Fertile agricultural land is often diverted for industrial projects, urban expansion, and infrastructure, leading to reduced crop output and ecological imbalance.
>
> • **Policy Role:** Government must enforce land-use policies that protect agricultural zones.
>
> • **HEI Role:** Conduct environmental and social impact assessments; advise on sustainable land-use planning.
>
> • **Impact:** Protects food security, sustains rural livelihoods, prevents ecological damage.

8\. International Partnerships and Collaborations

> • **What it means:** Global issues like food security, climate change, and agri-trade require cross-border knowledge and resource sharing. Collaborations with FAO, UNDP, CGIAR, and international HEIs can accelerate innovation.
>
> • **HEI Role:** Build partnerships for joint research, student exchanges, and collaborative projects in agriculture, health, and trade.
>
> • **Impact:** Enhanced research capacity, global exposure for students, faster adoption of global best practices in India.

**58. SDG-2.5 (Crop Gene Bank Preservation and Genetic Diversity)**

**Detailed Explanation of SDG-2.5 Recommendations**

1\. Strengthening National Gene Banks

> • India has already initiated gene banks under ICAR (National Bureau of Plant Genetic Resources, National Bureau of Animal Genetic Resources). However, current coverage is limited to a few major crops and domesticated animals.
>
> • There is a need to expand the scope to include:
>
> o Wild relatives of crops (e.g., wild rice, wild legumes, wild tubers).
>
> o Neglected and underutilized species important for climate resilience.
>
> o Local livestock breeds that contribute to nutrition and livelihood in niche areas.
>
> • Strengthened gene banks will serve as insurance against climate change, pests, and diseases.

2\. Survey of Local Farming Systems

> • A nationwide survey should be conducted by HEIs, agricultural universities, and local Krishi Vigyan Kendras (KVKs) to identify:
>
> o Traditional crops (millets, pulses, oilseeds).
>
> o Indigenous livestock breeds (cattle, goats, poultry).
>
> • These farming systems often rely on traditional knowledge and biodiversity that are otherwise overlooked in modern agriculture.
>
> • Community participation is essential: indigenous farmers can help maintain genetic diversity by continuing cultivation of these varieties.

3\. Documentation of Indigenous Knowledge (ITK & IAPs)

> • Indigenous Technical Knowledge (ITK) refers to centuries-old farming methods, soil management, water harvesting, and pest control systems.
>
> • Indigenous Agricultural Practices (IAPs) like intercropping, mixed farming, and natural pest management contribute to sustainability and resilience.
>
> • HEIs should help document, validate, and patent such knowledge, ensuring that communities are given due recognition and benefit sharing.
>
> • Example: Documentation of traditional seed storage methods can help create low-cost solutions for marginal farmers.

4\. Incentivizing Soil Organic Carbon (SOC) Enhancement

> • Maintaining genetic resources is linked with healthy soils, as soil biodiversity supports crop resilience.
>
> • Public and private initiatives should incentivize:
>
> o Green manuring, vermicomposting, and biochar use.
>
> o Carbon credits for farmers adopting SOC-enhancing practices.
>
> o Partnerships with private sector to promote sustainable soil management.
>
> • By improving SOC, we not only enhance soil fertility but also contribute to climate change mitigation through carbon sequestration.

**62. SDG–2 in the Economic Category**

From an economic perspective, SDG–2 (Zero Hunger) goes far beyond ensuring food security – it emphasizes agriculture as a driver of income generation, productivity, rural development, and market stability. Hunger and malnutrition reduce labour productivity, increase healthcare costs, and perpetuate cycles of poverty. Conversely, investment in sustainable agriculture, food systems, and nutrition yields high economic returns through job creation, higher farmer incomes, reduced food loss, and stabilized commodity markets.

Key dimensions include:

**1. Agricultural Productivity and Economic Growth**

> • Target 2.3 directly links to doubling the productivity and incomes of small-scale food producers (farmers, fishers, pastoralists).
>
> • Higher yields and diversified crops increase farm profitability, expand trade opportunities, and contribute to GDP growth.
>
> • HEIs can play a role by researching climate-smart technologies, AI-driven farming systems, and high-yield crop varieties to improve productivity.

**2. Food Security and Labour Efficiency**

> • Hunger and malnutrition reduce workforce efficiency. FAO estimates show that undernourishment lowers national productivity by up to 10% of GDP in low-income economies.
>
> • Investing in nutrition (biofortified crops, school meals, fortified food schemes) contributes to a healthier labour force and reduces public health expenditure.

**3. Value Addition, Post-Harvest Management, and Market Linkages**

> • Post-harvest losses in India are around 30–40% for perishables.
>
> • Reducing losses through value addition (processing, packaging, branding) creates new agri-business enterprises, enhances farm incomes, and expands export potential.
>
> • Promotion of FPOs (Farmer Producer Organizations) and agri-startups is crucial to integrate farmers into modern supply chains.

**4. Trade and Price Stability**

> • Targets 2.b and 2.c address agricultural trade restrictions and food price volatility.
>
> • Economic stability depends on fair trade policies, well-functioning markets (like e-NAM), and global competitiveness in exports.
>
> • HEIs can contribute by conducting policy research on trade reforms, WTO negotiations, and by building digital platforms for market price forecasting.

**5. Investment and Employment Generation**

> • Target 2.a highlights increasing investment in rural infrastructure, extension services, and agricultural R&D.
>
> • Agriculture remains the largest employer in countries like India (~40% workforce).
>
> • Rural youth employment can be expanded by promoting agripreneurship, food processing, supply chain management, and agri-tech startups.
>
> • International cooperation, funding, and private sector participation (through PPP models) strengthen agricultural capacity and resilience.

**HEI Role in Economic Category of SDG–2**

1\. Research & Development → Develop high-yield, biofortified, and climate-smart crops.

2\. Skill Development → Train youth in agri-business, post-harvest tech, and supply chain management.

3\. Entrepreneurship Support → Establish incubation centres for agri-startups.

4\. Policy Research → Provide evidence-based recommendations on trade, subsidies, and food security policies.

5\. Community Extension → Work with farmers, FPOs, and cooperatives to implement sustainable economic practices.

**Expected Economic Impacts**

> • Increased farm incomes and rural purchasing power.
>
> • Job creation in farming, food processing, and supply chains.
>
> • Reduction in food imports and improved trade balance through export-oriented crops.
>
> • Stabilized food prices benefiting both producers and consumers.
>
> • Higher contribution of agriculture to national GDP.

**69. Required Documents for QS I·GAUGE Related to SDG-2 (Zero Hunger)**

**Why QS I·GAUGE Cares about SDG-2**

QS I·GAUGE evaluates how higher education institutions contribute to the UN Sustainable Development Goals. For SDG-2 (Zero Hunger), it looks at whether HEIs are teaching, researching, and acting on food security, nutrition, and sustainable agriculture, while also measuring impact in society.

So, institutions are expected to show evidence — not just statements — across policies, academic activities, outreach, research, and impact.

**Explanation of Each Evidence Category**

1\. Policy & Governance

> • Institutions need an official policy or strategy for tackling hunger and food security, approved by their Governing Council or IQAC.
>
> • Why? Because this shows long-term commitment, not just ad hoc activities.

2\. Curriculum & Courses

> • If your HEI runs courses, electives, or MOOCs on topics like food security, nutrition, sustainable farming, or biofortification, you must provide syllabus copies, enrollment data, and certificates.
>
> • QS I·GAUGE wants to see how teaching directly supports SDG-2 awareness and skills.

3\. Research & Publications

> • Evidence of faculty/student research projects, publications, and patents related to hunger reduction, sustainable farming, or nutrition.
>
> • Shows that the HEI is creating knowledge that can solve real-world hunger challenges.

4\. Funding & Grants

> • Proof of government, international, or CSR funding received for SDG-2 projects.
>
> • For example: ICAR-funded food security research, CSR grants for farmer training.
>
> • This indicates resource mobilization capacity.

5\. Infrastructure & Labs

> • Documents proving the existence of nutrition labs, gene banks, demo farms, or seed preservation units.
>
> • HEIs are judged on their capacity to experiment and innovate in food-related research.

6\. Community Outreach

> • Reports and photos of food donation drives, mid-day meal support, POSHAN/Aanganwadi collaborations.
>
> • This category checks whether the HEI's impact goes beyond the campus to society.

7\. Food Waste Management

> • Evidence of policies and campaigns to minimize food waste in hostels, canteens, or events.
>
> • For example, audit reports, awareness posters, or "Zero Waste Campus" certificates.
>
> • Proves sustainability awareness.

8\. Student Engagement

> • Proof that student clubs and volunteers actively work on food security campaigns.
>
> • QS evaluates the extent of student participation, not just faculty involvement.

9\. Faculty Projects

> • Evidence of faculty consulting for NGOs/Govt on food systems, nutrition, and poverty alleviation.
>
> • This shows applied expertise that influences policies and practices.

**10. Employability & Incubation**

> • Startups or incubation projects in agri-tech, food processing, or sustainable nutrition must be documented.
>
> • QS emphasizes entrepreneurship and job creation linked to SDG-2.

**11. Alumni & CSR**

> • Alumni contributions (money, expertise, projects) towards food security must be shown.
>
> • This shows long-term community responsibility by graduates.

**12. International Partnerships**

> • MoUs or joint projects with UN agencies, FAO, World Food Programme, or foreign universities.
>
> • QS values global collaboration for food security.

**13. Impact Reports**

> • Outcome-oriented documentation, like malnutrition reduced in villages, number of families fed, or shelf-life extension research applied on farms.
>
> • This is proof of measurable change, not just activity.

**14. Digital Presence**

> • Institutions must showcase their SDG-2 initiatives online (official website, SDG dashboard).
>
> • QS gives importance to transparency and public accountability.

**15. Best Practices**

> • Case studies of successful food security interventions (like student-run food banks or farmer training programs).
>
> • This allows benchmarking and sharing institutional models globally.

**Why Evidence Matters**

> • Without evidence, claims don't count. QS I·GAUGE accreditation is data-driven.
>
> • Every activity — whether a seminar, policy, or project — must be backed with audited reports, photos, MoUs, or official links.
>
> • Institutions with structured documentation score higher, because it shows serious, measurable, and sustainable commitment.

**70. How ISO Standards Support SDG–2**

**1. Food Safety & Quality (Access to Safe Food)**

> • **ISO 22000:** Food Safety Management Systems – ensures food is safe “from farm to fork.”
>
> • **ISO/TS 22002:** Covers food production, storage, and distribution hygiene requirements.
>
> • **ISO 17025:** Competence of food testing laboratories, ensuring reliable results.

Support to SDG–2: Reduces contamination, ensures nutritional quality, and increases consumer trust.

**2. Sustainable Agriculture & Food Production**

> • **ISO 34101 series:** Sustainable and traceable cocoa production.
>
> • **ISO 14001:** Environmental Management – promotes eco-friendly farming practices.
>
> • **ISO 26000:** Social Responsibility – encourages ethical, inclusive food systems.
>
> • **ISO 50001:** Energy Management – improves energy efficiency in agri-food sectors.

Support to SDG–2: Encourages sustainable farming, optimizes resources (soil, water, energy), and reduces hunger through efficient production.

**3. Supply Chain & Food Security**

> • **ISO 28000:** Security in supply chains – ensures smooth, secure movement of food products.
>
> • **ISO 22005:** Traceability in feed and food supply chain – tracks food from producer to consumer.
>
> • **ISO 20400:** Sustainable procurement – ensures ethical sourcing of food products.

Support to SDG–2: Enhances global food distribution, reduces waste, and ensures equitable access.

**4. Nutrition, Packaging & Post-Harvest Loss Reduction**

> • **ISO 23415:** Packaging to reduce food waste and extend shelf-life.
>
> • **ISO 15189:** Clinical labs – supports nutrition-related health testing.
>
> • **ISO 21670:** Food products for special nutritional use (infants, elderly, patients).

Support to SDG–2: Extends food shelf-life, ensures nutritional adequacy, and supports vulnerable populations.

**5. Innovation, Research & Technology in Agriculture**

> • **ISO 19144 / ISO 19115:** Geospatial standards for land use & crop monitoring.
>
> • **ISO 35001:** Biorisk management for labs working on food and agriculture.
>
> • **ISO 37101:** Sustainable community development – links food security with smart cities.

Support to SDG–2: Promotes precision farming, climate-smart agriculture, and evidence-based policy making.
