**Abstract**

The Comprehensive Policy Framework for Innovation, Incubation, and Startup Development (IISP) in Higher Education Institutions (HEIs) serves as a strategic blueprint to foster a vibrant culture of innovation and entrepreneurship across academic ecosystems. It seeks to transform HEIs into dynamic centers for ideation, experimentation, and enterprise creation by establishing an integrated structure that connects education, research, and industry collaboration. The framework aligns with the National Innovation and Startup Policy (NISP 2019) and the National Education Policy (NEP 2020), ensuring that innovation becomes an integral component of institutional governance, curriculum design, and student engagement.

At its core, the IISP aims to nurture creative thinking, problem-solving, and entrepreneurial skills among students and faculty by embedding innovation-led learning through curricular, co-curricular, and extracurricular activities. The framework promotes the establishment of Innovation and Entrepreneurship Cells (IICs), Entrepreneurship Development Cells (EDCs), and Institutional Incubation Centres to provide infrastructural, technical, and financial support to innovators. These entities serve as facilitators for transforming novel ideas into viable products, services, or social enterprises through mentorship, pre-incubation, incubation, and acceleration support.

The policy also focuses on strengthening intellectual property management, ensuring protection and fair ownership of innovations through dedicated IPR Cells. It encourages faculty and students to engage in patent filing, licensing, and technology transfer to enhance the institution’s contribution to the national innovation ecosystem. Further, the framework underscores the importance of interdisciplinary collaboration, promoting innovation through hackathons, workshops, and joint research initiatives that connect multiple domains such as engineering, management, design, and social sciences.

A phased implementation plan ensures progressive development—short-term goals emphasize awareness and ideation, mid-term goals focus on intellectual property generation and funding mobilization, and long-term goals aim to create sustainable startups with measurable social and economic impact. The inclusion of Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) and benchmarking mechanisms linked to ARIIA, NIRF Innovation Rankings, and QS I-GAUGE ensures transparency, accountability, and continuous improvement in policy outcomes.

Ethical and social responsibility remain foundational to the IISP framework, which advocates for innovation for social good, alignment with UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), and the creation of inclusive entrepreneurial ecosystems. Ultimately, this policy positions HEIs as catalysts of economic growth and societal transformation, enabling them to evolve from centers of learning into engines of innovation, employment, and sustainable development in the 21st-century knowledge economy.

**1. Introduction**

The Comprehensive Policy Framework for Innovation, Incubation, and Startup Development (IISP) in Higher Education Institutions (HEIs) serves as a strategic institutional roadmap designed to nurture creativity, innovation, and entrepreneurship among students, faculty, and researchers. In the rapidly evolving global economy, innovation stands as a crucial driver of social and economic progress. HEIs, as centers of learning and research, play a pivotal role in fostering ideas and transforming them into viable enterprises. The IISP framework recognizes the importance of institutionalizing innovation processes to bridge the gap between knowledge creation and its practical application. It seeks to establish a structured ecosystem that supports the entire innovation lifecycle—from ideation and prototyping to incubation, commercialization, and startup growth.

Aligned with the National Innovation and Startup Policy (NISP 2019) and the National Education Policy (NEP 2020), this framework emphasizes the integration of entrepreneurship education into the curriculum at all levels—curricular, co-curricular, and extracurricular. It envisions transforming campuses into dynamic innovation hubs where students are not just learners but creators, inventors, and future entrepreneurs. Through this approach, HEIs become catalysts of sustainable development by contributing to economic growth, job creation, and societal advancement. The framework positions innovation not merely as a technical skill but as a mindset—an attitude that encourages critical thinking, problem-solving, and resilience among students and faculty alike.

A key feature of the IISP is its focus on developing an entrepreneurial ecosystem within HEIs. The establishment of dedicated structures such as Innovation and Entrepreneurship Cells (IICs), Entrepreneurship Development Cells (EDCs), and Institutional Incubation Centres provides a supportive environment where ideas can evolve into viable startups. These units serve as the nerve centers for ideation, mentorship, research commercialization, and networking with industry partners. They enable innovators to access infrastructure, funding, technical support, and guidance from experienced mentors and investors. By fostering collaboration between academia and industry, the framework ensures that innovations emerging from HEIs address real-world challenges and contribute meaningfully to the economy.

The policy also highlights the importance of interdisciplinary collaboration as a foundation for innovation. True innovation often emerges at the intersection of disciplines, where diverse perspectives converge to create new solutions. The IISP encourages HEIs to organize hackathons, idea marathons, and innovation boot camps that bring together students from engineering, management, science, design, and humanities to work on common problems. Such initiatives not only enhance teamwork and creativity but also promote a culture of innovation that transcends departmental boundaries. By encouraging inter-institutional collaboration, the policy aims to build a vibrant network of innovators across academic and professional domains.

Another essential component of the framework is capacity building through entrepreneurship-oriented pedagogy and experiential learning. The policy encourages institutions to introduce specialized training programs and short-term courses on innovation, intellectual property rights (IPR), design thinking, business model development, and financial management. Regular workshops, seminars, and guest lectures by entrepreneurs and industry experts are integral to developing the necessary skills and knowledge among students and faculty. By increasing exposure to government schemes, startup funding opportunities, and innovation grants, HEIs can empower aspiring entrepreneurs to transform their ideas into sustainable ventures.

The IISP further emphasizes the role of incubation centers in supporting startup development. Effective incubation requires access to infrastructure such as co-working spaces, laboratories, prototyping facilities, and testing centers. The policy advocates the establishment of Technology Business Incubators (TBIs) and Pre-Incubation Centres to assist innovators in developing and commercializing their products. It encourages HEIs to build partnerships with government agencies like DST, AICTE, MSME, and Startup India, as well as with private investors and venture capitalists. Such collaborations create a strong entrepreneurial pipeline that connects research, innovation, and market implementation.

In addition, intellectual property management forms a critical part of the IISP. Protecting and monetizing intellectual property is essential to ensure long-term innovation sustainability. The policy mandates the establishment of institutional IPR Cells to support patent filing, technology licensing, and knowledge transfer. It outlines guidelines for ownership rights, revenue sharing, and co-patenting between the institution and inventors. This not only motivates innovators but also promotes fairness and transparency in commercialization processes.

The framework adopts a phased implementation approach, focusing on short-term, mid-term, and long-term goals. In the short term, the policy focuses on awareness creation, capacity building, and ideation activities. The mid-term goals include enhancing IP generation, improving funding access, and supporting prototype development. The long-term vision is to create a self-sustaining ecosystem where startups thrive, contribute to employment, and achieve societal impact. This phased approach ensures progressive institutional growth and measurable outcomes.

Monitoring and evaluation are central to maintaining the policy’s effectiveness. The IISP introduces a Key Performance Indicator (KPI)-based monitoring system to assess progress objectively. Metrics such as the number of ideas generated, startups incubated, patents filed, and jobs created will be regularly tracked. These outcomes will guide policy refinement and resource allocation. Benchmarking against national and global innovation frameworks—such as ARIIA, NIRF Innovation Rankings, and QS I-GAUGE Innovation Metrics—ensures that institutions remain competitive and accountable.

The policy also embeds ethical and social responsibility into the innovation process. It emphasizes that all entrepreneurial activities must adhere to integrity, inclusivity, and sustainability. Startups emerging from HEIs are encouraged to align with the UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), particularly SDG 8 (Decent Work and Economic Growth), SDG 9 (Industry, Innovation, and Infrastructure), and SDG 17 (Partnerships for the Goals). By promoting technology-driven solutions for social welfare, rural development, and environmental conservation, the framework ensures that innovation serves as a tool for positive societal transformation.

Furthermore, the success of the IISP depends on institutional leadership and governance. A dedicated Innovation and Entrepreneurship Council (IEC), supported by senior administrators and faculty, will oversee policy implementation, monitor performance, and ensure inter-departmental coordination. Leadership commitment is essential to integrating innovation into the institution’s strategic vision, curriculum design, and faculty evaluation mechanisms. By treating innovation as a core institutional function, HEIs can sustain a culture that values creativity and enterprise.

In essence, the Comprehensive Policy Framework for Innovation, Incubation, and Startup Development represents a transformative shift in higher education—from knowledge transmission to knowledge creation, from job-seeking to job-making, and from passive learning to active problem-solving. It positions HEIs as dynamic ecosystems that inspire innovation, nurture entrepreneurship, and contribute to the broader goals of economic resilience and social progress. By institutionalizing innovation through structured interventions, capacity building, and measurable outcomes, this framework ensures that higher education becomes the driving force behind a new generation of innovators, entrepreneurs, and nation-builders.

**2. Innovation, Incubation and Startup Policy (IISP)**

**1. Preamble**

- In alignment with the National Innovation and Startup Policy (NISP–2019) of the Ministry of Education, Government of India, this institutional policy aims to foster innovation-driven entrepreneurship among students, faculty, and staff.

- The policy seeks to establish a sustainable ecosystem that nurtures ideation, incubation, and commercialization of innovative concepts within the campus environment.

**2. Policy Title**

- Innovation, Incubation and Startup Policy (IISP)

**3. Policy Statement**

- The institution is committed to promoting innovation, incubation, and startup culture by supporting creative ideas and translating them into viable products, processes, and enterprises that contribute to the national economy and societal development.

**4. Scope and Applicability**

- Applicable to all students, faculty members, research scholars, and staff of the institution.

- Covers activities related to idea generation, product design, prototype development, technology transfer, startup formation, and commercialization.

**5. Vision**

- To develop an inclusive and sustainable innovation ecosystem that empowers youth to transform ideas into successful enterprises, contributing to Atmanirbhar Bharat (Self-Reliant India).

**6. Mission**

- To build a robust institutional framework supporting innovation and entrepreneurship.

- To facilitate incubation support for idea validation and venture creation.

- To encourage interdisciplinary collaboration between academia, industry, and investors.

**7. Objectives**

- Promote innovation and entrepreneurial spirit among all academic and research communities.

- Support pre-incubation, incubation, and acceleration programs.

- Provide necessary infrastructure, mentoring, and funding assistance.

- Facilitate Intellectual Property Rights (IPR) management and technology transfer.

- Create awareness and build capacity in entrepreneurship, leadership, and business development.

**8. Governance Structure**

- Innovation and Entrepreneurship Council (IEC): Apex body to oversee policy implementation.

- Institution’s Innovation Cell (IIC): Operational body to conduct hackathons, ideathons, and innovation bootcamps.

- Incubation Centre: Provides co-working space, technical support, seed funding, and legal guidance.

- Startup Review Committee: Evaluates startup proposals and monitors progress.

**9. Implementation Mechanism**

- Establish Innovation Fund (minimum 1% of institutional annual budget).

- Develop physical and digital incubation infrastructure (Innovation Labs, FabLab, Tinkering Lab).

- Create a structured startup registration process for faculty and students.

- Facilitate mentorship programs with industry experts and alumni entrepreneurs.

- Organize annual startup expos, entrepreneurship summits, and investor meets.

**10. Resource Mobilization**

- Institutional funding support for prototype development and patent filing.

- External funding from DST, AICTE, MSME, Atal Innovation Mission, and CSR partnerships.

- Revenue generation through equity participation, licensing, and consultancy services.

**11. Incentives and Benefits**

- For Faculty:

<!-- -->

- Recognition in performance appraisal and promotion.

- Option for equity participation in startups.

- Provision of academic leave for entrepreneurial ventures.

<!-- -->

- For Students:

<!-- -->

- Academic credits for innovation projects/startup work.

- Flexible academic schedule to support incubation activities.

- Access to institutional labs and equipment.

**12. Intellectual Property Rights (IPR)**

- IPR generated through institutional resources shall be jointly owned by the inventor(s) and the institution.

- Revenue sharing model: Inventor – 70%, Institution – 30%.

- Establishment of IPR Cell for patent support, copyright registration, and awareness sessions.

**13. Capacity Building and Outreach**

- Conduct periodic entrepreneurship development programs (EDP), design thinking workshops, and business model training.

- Establish linkages with industry partners, angel investors, and venture capitalists.

- Promote rural and social entrepreneurship through community engagement.

**14. Monitoring and Evaluation**

- Annual review by Innovation Policy Implementation Committee (IPIC) under the IQAC framework.

- Key Performance Indicators (KPIs):

<!-- -->

- Number of ideas incubated.

- Startups launched.

- Patents filed and granted.

- Revenue and job creation metrics.

**15. Policy Review and Amendment**

- The policy shall be reviewed once every three years or as per changes in national guidelines.

- Modifications may be proposed by the Innovation and Entrepreneurship Council (IEC) and approved by the Governing Body.

**16. Alignment with National and Global Goals**

- Complies with NEP 2020 recommendations for innovation promotion in HEIs.

- Supports UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDG 8: Decent Work and Economic Growth; SDG 9: Industry, Innovation, and Infrastructure).

**17. Expected Outcomes**

- Establishment of a vibrant entrepreneurial ecosystem within the institution.

- Increase in faculty and student-led startups and patents.

- Enhanced industry-academia collaboration and employability.

- Contribution to local and national economic development.

**18. Approval and Enforcement**

- This policy shall come into force upon approval by the Board of Governors / Academic Council of the institution.

- The policy shall be disseminated through the institutional website and awareness programs.

This section outlines the norms, eligibility, and operational framework for faculty-led startups within the institution.

> • Such startups may be established individually or in collaboration with *students, faculty from other institutions, alumni, or external entrepreneurs.*
>
> • All faculty startups shall operate under the *ethical, legal, and academic guidelines* of the institution to ensure transparency, accountability, and alignment with institutional objectives.

**2 Institutional Support and Equity Participation**

- The institution may extend support to faculty-led startups in the form of:

**16. Code of Practice for Startups**

The Code of Practice serves as a guiding framework to eliminate ambiguity and ensure ethical, professional, and lawful conduct among all individuals involved in startup activities under the Innovation, Incubation and Startup Policy (IISP). **1 Objective of the Code of Practice**

- To define clear standards of behaviour and responsibility for faculty, students, and staff engaged in startups.

- To safeguard intellectual property rights (IPR), institutional reputation, and resource utilization.

- To ensure all startup-related activities are carried out with ethical integrity, transparency, and compliance with institutional and national regulations.

**2 Intellectual Property Rights (IPR) and Ownership**

\(a\) The institution shall be a co-owner of IPR for any product, technology, or innovation developed using its incubation facilities, financial assistance, or infrastructure.

\(b\) If the product or technology is developed outside institutional premises without using institutional resources, then the entire IPR shall rest solely with the creator or developer.

\(c\) All IPR ownership, licensing, and revenue-sharing arrangements must comply with the Institutional IPR Policy and be documented through formal agreements.

**3 Ethical Conduct and Anti-Corruption Norms**

\(d\) Faculty and students must not accept gifts, favours, or incentives in connection with startup activities, partnerships, or funding.

\(e\) No faculty or student shall bribe or attempt to influence any government, non-government, or private agency through gifts, entertainment, or unethical benefits.

\(f\) Any involvement of new personnel in the startup must be done only with prior approval from the Institutional Governance Committee (IGC) or Innovation and Entrepreneurship Council (IEC).

\(g\) All individuals must conduct themselves with honesty, respect, and discretion, fostering an environment of trust and professionalism.

**4 Confidentiality, Data Handling, and Approvals**

\(h\) Prior institutional approval is mandatory before accessing or using confidential, public, or sensitive data, or when activities involve human participants or live experiments.

\(i\) Faculty and students must uphold data confidentiality and privacy, ensuring that proprietary or sensitive information is not disclosed without authorization.

\(j\) The institution may conduct audits and monitoring of data management practices, and interference with the audit process is strictly prohibited.

**5 Resource Management and Integrity**

\(k\) Faculty and students must not misuse or waste institutional resources (funds, infrastructure, utilities, or manpower) for personal purposes.

\(l\) All resources allocated to the startup must be used solely for approved business activities and with proper documentation and accountability.

\(m\) Reliable testing frameworks and quality control mechanisms must be developed before launching any product or service to ensure safety, compliance, and credibility.

**6 Behavioural and Workplace Ethics**

\(n\) Faculty and students may participate in multiple startup ventures, provided they maintain strict confidentiality between ventures and avoid conflicts of interest.

\(o\) The personnel involved in startup activities are strictly prohibited from using, possessing, or distributing illegal drugs within institutional premises or during official events.

\(p\) Any form of violence, threat, harassment, or intimidation within institutional or incubation premises will not be tolerated and will lead to disciplinary action.

\(q\) All individuals must respect institutional rules, safety standards, and work culture, contributing to a safe, inclusive, and professional startup environment.

**7 Enforcement and Disciplinary Action**

\(r\) Any breach of this Code of Practice shall be taken seriously and reviewed by the Governance Council.

\(s\) The council may impose disciplinary actions such as suspension of incubation privileges, withdrawal of funding, or termination of startup registration, depending on the severity of the violation.

\(t\) The startup’s governance committee shall maintain a record of incidents, warnings, and corrective measures for internal audit and transparency.

**8 Expected Outcomes of Adhering to the Code of Practice**

- Promotion of ethical, disciplined, and transparent entrepreneurship.

- Strengthened trust among founders, mentors, investors, and the institution.

- Protection of institutional integrity, intellectual property, and public confidence.

- Creation of a safe, inclusive, and professional startup ecosystem aligned with national innovation and entrepreneurship values.

**18. Ethical and Conflict of Interest Guidelines**

Faculty members and students must ensure that their participation in startups upholds institutional values, promotes societal welfare, and prevents any misuse of institutional resources or positions.

**1 Objective of Ethical and Conflict of Interest Guidelines**

- To uphold the highest standards of integrity, transparency, and accountability in all startup-related activities.

- To ensure that faculty and student entrepreneurs operate in compliance with institutional rules and without personal or professional conflict.

- To protect the institution’s reputation, intellectual assets, and social responsibility while fostering innovation and entrepreneurship.

**2 Ethical and Operational Guidelines**

**(a) Employment Generation and Societal Benefit**

- Faculty and student entrepreneurs must ensure that their startup activities contribute to job creation and community development.

- Startups should align their goals with social responsibility, inclusivity, and sustainable impact.

- The focus should remain on value creation for society, not merely on profit generation.

**(b) Restriction on Personal Investment**

- The institution discourages faculty or students from making personal monetary investments in their own startups during the incubation phase.

- Financial involvement should follow institutional funding norms, grants, or approved seed fund channels.

- This policy ensures transparency in ownership structure and prevents conflicts of interest related to self-funding.

**(c) Avoidance of Conflict of Interest**

- Faculty members must ensure there is no conflict between their official academic or administrative responsibilities and their startup roles.

- Faculty participation in startups should not compromise teaching, research, or institutional commitments.

- Any potential conflict must be disclosed to the Innovation and Entrepreneurship Council (IEC) in advance and approved before proceeding.

**(d) Restriction on Duplicate Product or Service Development**

- Faculty and students engaged in startup activities should not work simultaneously on similar products or services within or outside the institution.

- This prevents intellectual property overlap, competitive duplication, or misuse of institutional resources.

- Any similar project development must be clearly differentiated and formally approved by the IPR Cell or Incubation Centre.

- Information disclosure to third parties should only occur on a need-to-know basis and with prior approval from the startup’s authorized committee or the institutional incubation office.

- Startups must maintain strict confidentiality agreements (NDAs) when engaging with external stakeholders such as investors, vendors, or collaborators.

- Conversely, all information relevant to the startup’s operation must be transparently shared with partners, mentors, and co-founders to ensure trust and alignment.

**20. Commercialization Guidelines**

The Innovation, Incubation and Startup Policy (IISP) emphasizes strategic commercialization as the bridge between innovation and market success. Once a startup has achieved significant technological maturity, it must make informed business decisions regarding its ownership structure, intellectual property management, and funding model. Commercialization ensures that innovations reach the market effectively while maintaining sustainability, profitability, and social value.

**1 Strategic Decision on Business Expansion**

Startup founders must choose their commercialization pathway carefully, based on market readiness, technology maturity, and risk appetite. The options are mutually exclusive, and each carries unique implications for ownership, funding, and intellectual property management:

**(i) Independent Expansion:**

- The startup expands while retaining full ownership and all rights.

- Suitable for ventures with sufficient internal funds or sustainable revenue models.

- Ensures complete control over decisions, branding, and intellectual property (IP).

**(ii) Equity-Based Expansion with IPR Retention:**

- The startup expands by offering equity shares to investors, partners, or collaborators but retains IP ownership with the founders or the institution.

- Suitable for startups requiring external funding for scaling operations while safeguarding their technological advantage.

**Decision Factors:**

> • **Market Risks –** Understanding demand volatility, competition, and scalability.
>
> • **Business Philosophy –** Balancing control with partnership opportunities.
>
> • **Stage of Technological Development –** Aligning investment needs with readiness levels.
>
> • **Potential Competitors –** Protecting innovation from replication or imitation.

**2 Guidelines for Commercialization Strategies**

**(a) Outsourcing Marketing While Retaining IPR**

- Once startups achieve notable technological progress or product maturity, they may outsource marketing, distribution, and customer acquisition activities to licensees or marketing partners.

- The startup shall retain all IPR rights, ensuring continuous ownership of the technology.

- Licensing agreements must include clear terms on royalties, confidentiality, and product representation.

**(b) Strategic Alliances with Immediate Customers**

- Startups may form strategic partnerships with early adopters or key customers who can act as angel investors, licensees, or pilot testers.

- These relationships help validate the product, build credibility, and generate early revenue streams.

- Every such partnership must be governed by a strong contractual agreement specifying roles, rights, revenue sharing, and intellectual property clauses.

**(c) Venture Capital and Equity Investment**

- Startups can attract venture capital (VC) investment where investors provide funding for product or service development in exchange for equity participation.

- Startups should ensure due diligence, transparency, and fair valuation before finalizing investment deals.

- The institution’s Incubation Centre or Legal Advisory Team may assist in reviewing VC agreements to protect startup interests and IPR ownership.

**(d) Bootstrapping Ecosystem Creation**

> • Startups are encouraged to adopt bootstrapping — reinvesting early profits into business expansion rather than relying solely on external funding.

- This fosters financial discipline, sustainability, and independence.

- Bootstrapping also builds credibility, making the startup more attractive to future investors or partners.

**(e) Reinvestment for Diversification and Growth**

- Well-established startups shall use profits from existing products or services to fund the commercialization of new innovations.

- This ensures continuous innovation, product diversification, and long-term financial growth.

- The approach encourages a culture of reinvestment and innovation recycling, keeping the startup competitive and relevant.

**3 Institutional Support in Commercialization**

- The institution shall provide advisory and legal assistance to startups during the commercialization phase, covering:

<!-- -->

- IPR licensing agreements and royalty models.

- Investment negotiations and due diligence.

- Strategic marketing collaborations and alliance frameworks.

<!-- -->

- The Technology Transfer Office (TTO) and IPR Cell shall jointly oversee all commercialization contracts to ensure compliance with institutional and legal standards.

**4 Expected Outcomes of Commercialization**

**27. Evaluation Metrics and Performance Indicators**

The *Innovation, Incubation and Startup Policy (IISP)* establishes a structured evaluation framework to measure the progress, effectiveness, and long-term impact of institutional innovation and entrepreneurship initiatives. The evaluation will be conducted across short-term, mid-term, and long-term durations, ensuring continuous improvement and accountability of the ecosystem.

**1 Objective of Evaluation Framework**

- To assess the growth and maturity of the innovation and startup ecosystem within the institution.

- To quantify outcomes related to student participation, faculty engagement, innovation outputs, and commercialization success.

- To provide evidence-based insights for policy review, strategic planning, and resource allocation.

**2 Evaluation Structure and Timeline**

The assessment framework is divided into three stages:

> • **Short-Term (1 Year):** Measures awareness, participation, and early innovation outcomes.
>
> • **Mid-Term (2 Years):** Evaluates intellectual property, commercialization, and funding performance.
>
> • **Long-Term (3 Years):** Focuses on startup success, infrastructure development, and societal impact.

**3 Short-Term Activities (One-Year Duration)**

Evaluation indicators for the first year will focus on participation, ideation, and awareness-building:

\(i\) Number of students and faculty who participated in awareness sessions, entrepreneurship training, or innovation programs organized by the HEI.

\(ii\) Number of students and faculty who participated in innovation or startup-related events organized by external agencies (e.g., AICTE, DST, MSME, Startup India).

\(iii\) Number of ideas generated and innovations created through ideation programs, hackathons, and innovation challenges.

\(iv\) Number of innovative projects recognized at national or international levels through awards or competitions.

\(v\) Number of ideas, innovations, or startups funded by the Startup and Business Innovation Cell (SBIC) or equivalent institutional mechanism.

\(vi\) Number of academic courses introduced on Innovation, IPR, and Entrepreneurship across departments.

**4 Mid-Term Activities (Two-Year Duration)**

The second-year evaluation shall focus on tangible innovation outcomes, IP achievements, and funding mobilization:

\(i\) Number of intellectual properties (IP) filed or provisionally granted by students, faculty, or incubated startups.

\(ii\) Number of patents granted nationally or internationally.

\(iii\) Number of technology transfers or commercializations successfully executed between startups, industries, and research partners.

\(iv\) Total grants or funds generated or received by the institution to support innovation and startup initiatives (including central/state funding).

\(v\) Angel funds, venture funds, or private investments mobilized to support innovation and startup ventures within the institution.

**5 Long-Term Programs (Three-Year Duration)**

The three-year evaluation will measure ecosystem maturity, infrastructure expansion, and long-term societal contributions:

\(i\) Number of successful innovations converted into functional startups operating independently or in collaboration with industry.

\(ii\) Number of advanced laboratory infrastructures, pre-incubation and incubation facilities established or upgraded on campus.

\(iii\) Number of innovative solutions developed in-house and their impact levels after being adopted within the institute or by society (e.g., local governance, MSMEs, NGOs).

**6 Implementation and Monitoring Mechanism**

- The Institution Innovation Council (IIC) and Entrepreneurship Development Cell (EDC) shall coordinate periodic evaluations and submit reports to the Innovation and Entrepreneurship Council (IEC).

- Evaluation results will be reviewed annually to track progress against set KPIs.

- A dashboard-based tracking system will be developed to record, analyze, and visualize data across all indicators.

- Key findings shall inform strategic revisions of policy, training programs, and funding allocation.

**7 Expected Outcomes of the Evaluation Framework**

- Establishment of a data-driven performance culture for innovation and entrepreneurship.

- Continuous improvement in startup quality, mentorship efficiency, and IP management.

- Enhanced visibility of the institution in national innovation rankings (e.g., ARIIA, NIRF Innovation).

- Increased funding, collaborations, and technology transfers through measurable success indicators.

- Long-term institutional impact contributing to economic growth and social development.

**28. Benchmarking, Key Performance Indicators (KPIs), Monitoring, and Evaluation Framework**

The *Innovation, Incubation and Startup Policy (IISP)* establishes a comprehensive Benchmarking and KPI-based Monitoring Framework to ensure that institutional innovation and entrepreneurship activities are aligned with measurable outcomes, global standards, and continuous improvement principles. This framework acts as the foundation for performance accountability, transparency, and strategic decision-making across all levels of the innovation ecosystem.

**1 Objectives of Benchmarking and KPI Framework**

- To define quantifiable performance benchmarks for innovation, incubation, and startup development.

- To track institutional progress against national and international innovation indicators.

- To foster a culture of data-driven evaluation, evidence-based policy improvement, and strategic alignment.

- To identify strengths, gaps, and opportunities for institutional and ecosystem growth.

**2 Benchmarking Standards**

The institution shall adopt benchmarking mechanisms that align with recognized national and international innovation frameworks, including:

> • **Atal Ranking of Institutions on Innovation Achievements (ARIIA) –** for assessing institutional innovation performance.
>
> • **National Innovation and Startup Policy (NISP) –** for governance and implementation benchmarks.
>
> • **National Institutional Ranking Framework (NIRF) – Innovation Category –** for national competitiveness.
>
> • **QS I-GAUGE Innovation Parameters –** for international benchmarking and quality certification.
>
> • **UN Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs 8, 9, and 17) –** for linking entrepreneurship with inclusive and sustainable growth.

Each institutional unit (e.g., IIC, EDC, SBIC, and Incubation Centre) shall use these benchmarks to set yearly performance targets and align their activities with policy outcomes.

**3 Key Performance Indicators (KPIs)**

**(a) Input Indicators**

Measure the resources and capacity built by the institution to support innovation and entrepreneurship:

- % of annual budget allocated to innovation and startup initiatives.

- Number of faculty and staff trained in innovation and incubation management.

- Number of functional innovation and incubation facilities.

- Total funds mobilized from government, industry, and investors.

**(b) Process Indicators**

Measure the efficiency and implementation of innovation processes:

- Number of ideation, hackathon, or innovation events organized.

- Number of mentorship hours provided and active mentor engagements.

- Number of entrepreneurship development workshops conducted.

- Timeliness and efficiency in processing startup applications and funding support.

**(c) Output Indicators**

Measure the immediate outcomes of innovation and startup activities:

- Number of ideas converted into prototypes or proofs of concept.

- Number of student/faculty startups incubated annually.

- Number of patents filed, granted, or commercialized.

- Number of products launched or services deployed.

**(d) Outcome Indicators**

Evaluate the impact and sustainability of innovation outcomes:

- Number of startups successfully graduated and operational.

- Total employment generated by institutional startups.

- Startup revenue, export potential, or investment raised.

- Number of social innovations and community impact initiatives.

**(e) Impact Indicators**

Measure long-term ecosystem and societal benefits:

- Contribution to local and national economy.

- Institutional ranking improvement in ARIIA/NIRF Innovation Index.

- Partnerships developed with industry, government, and academia.

- Visible social, environmental, and technological advancements achieved.

**4 Monitoring and Evaluation Mechanism**

\(a\) A dedicated Monitoring and Evaluation Committee (MEC) shall be established under the Innovation and Entrepreneurship Council (IEC).

\(b\) The MEC shall meet quarterly to review progress, analyze data, and generate performance dashboards.

\(c\) Each sub-unit (IIC, EDC, SBIC, and Incubator) shall submit quarterly KPI reports highlighting activities, outcomes, and challenges.

\(d\) Annual performance audits will be conducted by the Institutional Quality Assurance Cell (IQAC) to ensure compliance and alignment with institutional goals.

\(e\) The findings from evaluation reports will be shared with the Governing Council and Board of Management for strategic policy revisions.

**5 Benchmark-Based Continuous Improvement Model**

The institution shall adopt a Plan–Do–Check–Act (PDCA) model to ensure consistent quality enhancement and innovation performance:

1\. **Plan:** Set measurable KPIs and annual performance targets.

2\. **Do:** Implement innovation and startup activities as per policy directives.

3\. **Check:** Evaluate outcomes through monitoring dashboards and KPI analysis.

4\. **Act:** Modify strategies, reallocate resources, and improve processes based on feedback.

This cyclical model ensures that the ecosystem remains adaptive, scalable, and globally competitive.

**6 Reporting and Transparency**

- All KPI results and evaluation findings will be compiled into an Annual Innovation and Startup Performance Report (AISPR).

- The report will be made accessible to stakeholders, accreditation agencies, and government innovation bodies.

- A summary of outcomes shall be displayed on the institution’s innovation portal to maintain transparency and encourage stakeholder participation.

**7 Expected Outcomes of KPI-Based Evaluation**

- Establishment of a robust and transparent monitoring system for innovation and entrepreneurship.

- Enhanced accountability, efficiency, and stakeholder engagement.

- Improved ranking and recognition in national innovation indexes (ARIIA, NIRF).

- Strengthened funding opportunities through data-backed performance evidence.

- Creation of a globally benchmarked innovation-driven institution.
