**Abstract**

Higher Education Institutions (HEIs) have adopted a structured and dynamic academic system designed to ensure effective delivery, governance, and enhancement of educational programs. This system provides a holistic framework that integrates the key dimensions of academic operations—ranging from curriculum design to evaluation, progression, quality assurance, and academic leadership.

At the core of this system is a well-defined curriculum structure, which includes a balanced mix of core courses, discipline-specific electives, open electives, laboratory and studio-based work, skill enhancement courses, internships, and value-added or community engagement modules. The structure is closely aligned with the Program Educational Objectives (PEOs), Program Outcomes (POs), and Course Outcomes (COs), ensuring that the academic experience is outcome-oriented. Many institutions follow the Choice Based Credit System (CBCS) or Outcome-Based Education (OBE) model, which provides students the flexibility to design their learning paths by selecting courses from a wide pool of options based on interest, career goals, and individual pace. Credits are assigned based on instructional hours, difficulty level, and expected learning effort.

The academic system is governed through a multi-tier governance model, with the Academic Council functioning as the highest academic decision-making body responsible for reviewing regulations, maintaining academic standards, approving new programs, and research, and administrative responsibilities. Operational guidelines are well-documented and implemented institution-wide. These include the timely release of the Academic Calendar, facilitation of course registration each semester, and transparent documentation of faculty workload, which covers teaching, mentoring, seminars, institutionalized to promote holistic development and exposure beyond the classroom.

Supporting this, the Boards of Studies (BoS) at the departmental level handle curriculum development, syllabus revision, and course relevance, often incorporating industry and alumni feedback. The Internal Quality Assurance Cell (IQAC) plays a pivotal role in ensuring continuous quality improvement, aligning institutional practices with national accreditation standards such as NAAC and NBA, and promoting a culture of excellence and accountability.

The assessment and evaluation component of the system is robust and includes a combination of Continuous Internal Evaluation (CIE) and Semester End Examinations (SEE). CIE involves formative assessments such as quizzes, assignments, project work, presentations, and mid-term tests, while SEE evaluates students’ cumulative understanding at the end of the semester. The grading process is governed by either an absolute or relative grading scale, typically mapped to a 10-point CGPA system, with transparency and fairness being key principles. Institutions may also employ formative, summative, diagnostic, and self/peer assessment methods to support deeper learning and reflective practices.

Student progression is guided by clearly stated academic regulations that define the criteria for promotion to the next semester or year, including minimum CGPA thresholds, satisfactory attendance (usually 75% or higher), and completion of prerequisite courses. To promote employability and real-world readiness, the system integrates Massive Open Online Courses (MOOCs), Skill Enhancement Courses (SECs), industry internships, and community service or field-based projects as part of the academic structure. These components enable students to acquire transferable skills, engage with practical challenges, and enhance their overall learning experience.

The academic system also supports specialized programs, such as Honours and Minor degrees, which allow students to pursue in-depth study in their core discipline (Honours) or explore interdisciplinary domains (Minors) by earning additional credits. These optional tracks promote academic differentiation and provide competitive advantages in both higher education and employment. Additionally, the conduct of workshops, professional society activities, and extension lectures is institutionalized to promote holistic development and exposure beyond the classroom.

In summary, the academic system followed by HEIs is a comprehensive, flexible, and student-centric ecosystem. It integrates quality assurance mechanisms, regulatory compliance, and academic innovation to ensure that students graduate with strong disciplinary knowledge, essential life skills, and the capacity to thrive in a global, knowledge-driven economy.

**1. Introduction**

This document comprehensively outlines the academic system adopted by Higher Education Institutions (HEIs) to plan, execute, and evaluate their academic programs with transparency, consistency, and quality. It provides a holistic and structured framework encompassing all critical academic components, ensuring institutions meet the expectations of students, industry, and regulatory bodies while fostering academic excellence and innovation. At the heart of this academic framework lies the Credit-Based System, which forms the foundation for curriculum planning and student evaluation. Most institutions adopt either the Choice Based Credit System (CBCS) or the Outcome-Based Education (OBE) model. These models promote student autonomy and flexibility by allowing learners to select from a wide range of elective, core, and interdisciplinary courses. **1.1. Definition and Scope**

• Refers to the structured framework adopted by Higher Education Institutions (HEIs) to plan, deliver, and assess academic programs.

• Includes curricula, teaching-learning processes, assessment systems, grading, progression criteria, and academic governance.

**1.2. Key Components**

• **Curriculum Structure:** Comprises core, elective, lab, skill, internship, and value-added courses aligned with program objectives and outcomes.

• **Credit-Based System:** Most HEIs follow a Choice Based Credit System (CBCS) or Outcome-Based Education (OBE), where each course is assigned credits based on hours and difficulty.

• **Academic Calendar:** A pre-published schedule detailing instructional days, examination periods, vacation, and result declaration.

• **Course Delivery:** Through lectures, tutorials, practicals, projects, seminars, and internships using blended learning approaches.

• **Assessment:** Comprises Continuous Internal Evaluation (CIE) and Semester End Examination (SEE) with defined weightages.

• **Grading System:** Relative or absolute grading based on student performance; typically mapped to a 10-point CGPA scale.

• **Examinations and Evaluation:** Transparent and structured evaluation methods with moderation, revaluation, and grievance redressal provisions.

• **Student Progression:** Criteria defined for promotion to higher semesters, including attendance, minimum CGPA, and completion of prerequisites.

**1.3. Governance and Quality Assurance**

• **Academic Council:** Apex academic authority overseeing quality, curriculum, assessment, and academic standards.

• Boards of Studies (BoS): Department-level bodies responsible for course design, review, and content relevance.

• **Standing Committees:** Handle urgent academic decisions between regular council meetings.

• Internal Quality Assurance Cell (IQAC): Ensures continuous improvement and compliance with accreditation norms (e.g., NAAC, NBA).

**1.4. Supportive Initiatives**

• MOOCs & Skill Courses: Integration of online and interdisciplinary skill-based courses to enhance employability.

• Internships & Community Projects: Mandated experiential learning through industrial exposure or social engagement.

• Value Added & Mandatory Courses: Encourage holistic development beyond the core curriculum.

**1.5. Regulatory Compliance**

• Conforms to UGC, AICTE, and respective university/affiliating body norms for autonomy, curriculum revision, and academic policies.

**4. Academic Council**

**4.1 Introduction**

The Academic Council is the principal academic body of the Institute. Subject to the provisions of the Memorandum of Association, Rules, and Bye-Laws, it holds the responsibility for maintaining the standards of education, teaching, and training within the Institute. It ensures effective coordination among departments, supervises research activities, and oversees the conduct of examinations and assessments. In addition, the Council exercises all other powers and performs duties as may be assigned to it under the Institute's Rules and Bye-Laws, thereby ensuring the academic integrity and progress of the institution.

**1. Definition and Role**

• Highest academic authority in a university or higher education institution

• Responsible for the control, direction, and regulation of academic matters

**2. Functions**

• Formulation and approval of academic policies and frameworks

• Design, approval, and revision of curricula and syllabi

• Oversight of teaching, learning, research, and examination systems

• Recommendation of guidelines for academic quality and assessment

• Promotion of research, innovation, and interdisciplinary programs

• Granting recognition to academic programs and external collaborations

**3. Composition**

• **Chairperson:** Vice-Chancellor / President

• **Members:**

> o Deans of Faculties / Schools
>
> o Heads of Departments (HoDs)
>
> o Senior Professors / Eminent Scholars
>
> o External Experts (industry or academia)
>
> o Registrar (Secretary or Member Secretary)
>
> o Controller of Examinations (ex-officio)
>
> o Librarian, Directors of R&D, IQAC, and Student Affairs (as needed)

**4. Meetings and Procedures**

• Typically meets once or twice per semester

• Agenda circulated in advance; minutes recorded and approved

• Decisions are binding on academic departments after ratification

**5. Impact and Governance Link**

• Acts as an advisory body to the Vice-Chancellor and Senate

• Bridges governance with academic execution

• Ensures compliance with national accreditation standards (e.g., NAAC, UGC)

**4.2. Core Functions of the Academic Council**

**1. Academic Policy and Governance**

• Formulate and implement academic policies, frameworks, and regulations

• Serve as the principal academic authority of the university

• Approve academic statutes, byelaws, and guidelines

**2. Curriculum Oversight**

• Approve new programs, courses, and specializations proposed by Boards of Studies

• Review and ratify curriculum structures, syllabi, and academic calendars

• Ensure alignment with national education policies, accreditation norms, and OBE standards

**3. Research and Innovation Promotion**

• Encourage faculty and student research initiatives

• Approve guidelines for thesis/dissertation, publication, and ethics in research

• Review research progress reports and recommend improvements

**4. Examination and Evaluation Control**

• Set academic standards for examinations, grading, and assessment methods

• Oversee regulations for conduct of internal and external exams

• Approve appointment of examiners, moderators, and evaluators

• Ensure academic integrity and fairness in evaluation systems

**5. Quality Assurance and Academic Standards**

• Promote continuous improvement in teaching-learning processes

• Set standards for course outcomes, credit systems, and academic audits

• Facilitate accreditation, quality benchmarks, and ranking preparedness

**6. Recognition and Equivalence**

• Recognize qualifications from other universities/institutes for admissions and credit transfers

• Determine equivalence of degrees, diplomas, and certificate programs

**7. Institutional Development**

• Recommend establishment or closure of schools, departments, centers, or interdisciplinary units

• Approve collaborations with national/international academic bodies

• Recommend policies on student conduct, scholarships, and fellowships

**8. Committee and Sub-Committee Engagement**

• Constitute academic committees/sub-committees for specific domains (e.g., curriculum, assessment, innovation)

• Review reports and proposals submitted by such committees

**4.3. Roles and Responsibilities of the Academic Council**

**1. General Role**

• Oversees, supervises, and governs all academic operations and policies

**2. Academic Oversight**

• Exercises general supervision over academic activities

• Provides guidance on teaching methods, research practices, evaluation systems, and academic improvement

• Promotes and reviews research activities conducted within the university

• Initiates or considers academic matters independently or as referred by the Board of Management

**3. Curriculum & Examination Management**

• Prescribes academic programs leading to degrees, diplomas, and certificate courses

• Conducts examinations in alignment with university byelaws

• Maintains and enhances standards of examination and evaluation

• Appoints examiners, moderators, tabulators, and other examination-related personnel

**4. Academic Policy and Recognition**

• Recognizes internal university credentials and determines equivalence with other institutions

• **Recommends bye-laws related to:**

> o Academic functioning
>
> o Discipline, attendance, and student conduct
>
> o Admissions, examinations, fellowships, and concessions

**5. Quality, Fellowship & Institutional Development**

• **Recommends:**

> o Improvement of teaching, training, and research standards
>
> o Establishment or closure of departments, divisions, centers, and schools
>
> o Institution of scholarships, medals, fellowships, and awards
>
> o Creation of teaching posts (Professors, Readers, Lecturers)

**6. Coordination & Advisory Functions**

• Suggests strategies for inter-divisional coordination

• Appoints sub-committees to advise on specific academic issues

• Reviews sub-committee recommendations and proposes actions to the Board

• Periodically reviews performance of academic divisions/centers and advises on improvements

**4.4. Powers of the Academic Council**

**General Supervision and Leadership**

• Exercises overall supervision over academic policies of the college

• Provides leadership to enhance standards and quality of education and research

**Annual Report Oversight**

• Has the authority to consider and review the Annual Report

**Appointment and Delegation**

• Can appoint Committees and delegate powers to such Committees or the Principal

• Delegated powers pertain to functions assigned by UGC regulations or college rules

**Meeting Frequency and Annual Session**

• Meets at least two times a year to handle regular academic affairs

• One meeting designated as the Annual Meeting, to be held before 31st March for presentation of the Annual Report

**Standing Committee to Academic Council**

• A Standing Committee may act on behalf of the Academic Council when not in session

• Actions of the Standing Committee are subject to ratification in the subsequent Academic Council meeting

**Consultative Powers**

• Can seek opinions from any Faculty or Board of Studies on academic matters

**Governance and Procedure**

• Follows procedures as per UGC/AICTE regulations and rules framed by the college for conducting meetings of both the Academic Council and its Standing Committee

**Rule-making Authority**

• Empowered to formulate detailed rules governing all academic programs

**4.5. Chairing of Academic Council Meetings**

> o The principal shall preside over all meetings of the Academic Council, if present.
>
> o In the absence of the Principal, the Vice Principal or Dean Academics shall chair the meeting.
>
> o If neither is available, the Academic Council shall elect a member from among themselves to preside over the meeting.

**4.6. Quorum for Academic Council Meetings**

• **Minimum Attendance Requirement:**

One-third (1/3) of the *total sanctioned strength* of the Academic Council, including the Chairperson, must be present to constitute a valid quorum for any official meeting.

• **Inclusion of Chairperson:**

> o The Chairperson (normally the principal) is included in the count toward the quorum.

• **Responsibility to Ensure Quorum:**

> o It is the duty of the Member Secretary (typically the Dean Academics or designated official) to confirm quorum before commencement of the meeting.

• **Lack of Quorum:**

> o If the quorum is not met within 30 minutes from the scheduled time:

• The meeting stands adjourned automatically.

• The adjourned meeting may be reconvened at a later date or time as decided by the Chairperson.

• At the reconvened meeting, the agenda may be transacted with the members present, if such a provision exists in the institutional byelaws.

• **Special Circumstances:**

> o For emergency meetings, written consent from at least one-third of the members is necessary to call and conduct the meeting.

• **Recording of Attendance:**

> o Attendance of all members present shall be recorded and maintained in official minutes as evidence of compliance with quorum requirements.

• **Decision Validity:**

Any decisions made in a meeting lacking quorum shall be considered *invalid* and subject to ratification in a properly convened meeting.

• **Communication:**

> o Notice of meeting, agenda, and quorum requirements shall be communicated to all members at least seven days prior to the meeting date (except in urgent cases).

**4.7. Notice of the Meeting**

• **Issuing Authority:**

> o The Principal Office is responsible for issuing the notice.

• **Authority and Oversight:**

> o The notice is issued under the direction of the principal, ensuring institutional compliance.

• **Notice Period:**

> o A minimum of fifteen (15) days’ notice must be given prior to the scheduled date of the meeting.

• **Purpose:**

> o To inform Academic Council members about the meeting well in advance for adequate preparation and participation.

• **Contents of the Notice:**

> o Date, time, and venue of the meeting.
>
> o Detailed agenda or topics to be discussed.
>
> o Any preparatory material or attachments relevant to the meeting.

• **Mode of Communication:**

> o The notice may be delivered via email, printed circular, or posted on official notice boards, depending on institutional practice.

• **Urgent Meetings Exception:**

> o In case of urgent/emergency meetings, the notice period may be reduced with proper justification and approval from the principal.

**4.10. Order of Business – Academic Council Meetings**

• **Purpose:**

> o To maintain a structured and prioritized sequence for addressing academic matters during each meeting.

• **Standard Sequence of Agenda Items:**

a\. Business from the Governing Body

> o Items referred or recommended by the Governing Body.
>
> o Includes policy decisions, institutional directives, or governance-level matters.

b\. Business from the Principal

> o Institutional-level academic initiatives.
>
> o Reports, updates, proposals, or actions requiring council approval.

c\. Business from Faculties and Boards of Studies (BoS)

> o Curriculum revisions, course approvals, assessment modifications.
>
> o Academic improvements and quality assurance proposals from departments.

d\. Business from Members (Motions)

> o Proposals or suggestions submitted by individual Academic Council members.
>
> o Must adhere to submission guidelines (e.g., advance notice).

• **Additional Guidelines:**

> o All items must be included in the agenda paper circulated before the meeting.
>
> o Chairperson’s discretion may be used to reorder or prioritize items in case of urgency.
>
> o Time allocation may be determined based on the importance and complexity of the item.

• **Objective:**

> o To ensure efficient, inclusive, and transparent academic governance.

**4.11. How to Move Motions – Academic Council Meetings**

• **Format of Motion:**

> o Every motion must be affirmative in form (i.e., propose an action or decision clearly and positively).

• **Procedure:**

> o A motion must be moved by a member of the Academic Council.
>
> o It must be seconded by another member during the meeting.
>
> o If no seconder is present, the motion is deemed dropped and will not be considered.

• Exceptions (No Seconding Required):

> o Motions placed by the Governing Body.
>
> o **Motions placed by the principal on behalf of:**
>
> o College Authorities
>
> o Official administrative or academic committees

• **Purpose:**

> o To ensure only proposals with minimum support are discussed.
>
> o To maintain orderly and efficient proceedings of the Academic Council.

**4.12. Order of Amendments – Academic Council Meetings**

1\. Chairperson’s Authority:

> o The Chairperson determines the sequence in which amendments to any resolution are to be moved and discussed.

2\. **Procedure after Debate:**

> o Once the debate on a proposed amendment concludes, the Chairperson shall:
>
> o **Announce:** “It has been moved” and state the original motion.

• **Then announce:** “Since then it has been moved by way of amendment” and clearly state the proposed amendment.

• **Purpose:**

> o To maintain clarity in proceedings.
>
> o To ensure members understand both the original motion and any modifications proposed through amendments.

**4.13. Rule: Limit on Resolutions and Amendments at a Time**

• **Single Motion Rule:**

> o At any given time, only one resolution and one amendment to it may be presented by a member during an Academic Council meeting.

• **Procedure if Amendment is Passed:**

> o If an amendment is approved, the Chairperson shall:

• State the amended motion.

• Treat it as a new substantive motion open for further discussion.

• **Subsequent Amendments:**

> o Additional amendments may be moved only if they relate to the amended motion and are in order.
>
> o These new amendments shall be handled in the same manner as the original.

• **Final Voting:**

> o Once all amendments are addressed, the Chairperson shall:

• Put the final amended motion to vote as the substantive motion.

**4.14. Withdrawal of Motions at the Meeting**

• **Unanimous Consent Required:**

> o A resolution or amendment cannot be withdrawn during the meeting without unanimous consent of the members present.

• **Procedure with Proposed Amendment:**

> o **If an amendment has been proposed to a resolution:**

• The original motion cannot be withdrawn until the amendment has been formally disposed of (i.e., accepted or rejected).

**4.15. Ruling Out of Order a Motion**

• **Authority of the Chairperson:**

> o The Chairperson has the right to rule a motion out of order at any stage during the proceedings of the Academic Council.

• **Timing of Ruling:**

> o This ruling can occur any time before the resolution is put to vote.

• **Purpose:**

> o Ensures that the proceedings remain relevant, orderly, and within the scope of the Council’s rules and agenda.

**4.16. Motion for Appointment of a Committee**

• **Timing of Motion:**

> o May be made at any time, provided it does not interrupt a speech in progress.

• **Contents of the Motion:**

> o **Must clearly state:**

• The purpose for which the committee is being constituted.

• The names of its members.

• The name of the convener of the committee.

• **Permissible Amendments:**

> o **Amendments to the motion may be proposed to:**

• Enlarge or restrict the scope/purpose of the committee.

• Modify the question or subject remitted to the committee.

**4.17. When Speeches Are Allowed**

• **General Rule:**

> o A member may speak only when there is a question before the meeting or when moving or seconding a motion.

• **Exceptions:**

> o A member is allowed to speak even without a motion in the following cases:

• a) When speaking to a point of order

• b) When offering a personal explanation

• c) When, with special permission of the Chair, making a formal statement

• **Pre-Motion Speech:**

> o A member may speak before moving a motion they intend to propose
>
> o **However:**

• The speech must be limited to the question at hand

• The member must conclude by formally moving the motion

**4.18. How Often Speeches Are Permitted**

• **One-Time Rule:**

> o A member may not speak more than once on the same question, unless specifically permitted.

• **Restriction on Amendment Participation:**

> o **A member who has already spoken on the main question:**

• May not move or second an amendment to it during the ongoing debate.

• May speak on an amendment if it is moved and seconded by others, provided debate is allowed.

• **Special Provision:**

> o A member may, with the special permission of the Chair, make a statement on any matter arising from the debate.

**4.19. Conditions for the Right of Reply for the Mover**

• **Right of Reply Timing:**

> o The mover of the resolution may exercise the right to reply only after the Chairperson confirms that no other member wishes to speak on the question.

• **Scope of Reply:**

> o The reply may address the entire debate, summarizing or responding to the points raised.

• **Closure of Debate:**

> o No further discussion or speeches shall be allowed after the mover's reply has been delivered.

**4.20. Duration of Speeches**

• **Standard Duration:**

> o No speech shall exceed five minutes in duration during meetings.

• **Exception for Movers:**

> o The mover of a resolution or amendment may be allowed to speak longer, subject to permission from the Chairperson.

• Chairperson's Discretion:

> o The Chairperson determines the permissible extension of time for speeches beyond the standard limit

**4.21. Order of Speeches**

• **Right to Speak:**

> o The member who first rises to speak at the end of a previous speech shall have the right to be heard.

• **Simultaneous Requests:**

> o If more than one member rises simultaneously, the Chairperson shall decide who may speak first.

• Chairperson's Role:

> o The Chairperson maintains order and ensures fair opportunity for members to participate in the discussion.

**4.22. Personal Explanation**

• **Right to Clarify:**

> o **A member may make a personal explanation if:**

• Their speech has been misunderstood, or

• Their conduct or character has been impugned during the debate.

• **Timing of Explanation:**

> o The explanation may be given even while another member is speaking, but only with the Chairperson’s permission.

• **Purpose:**

> o The explanation should be strictly limited to clarifying the misunderstanding or addressing the impugned matter.

**4.23. Point of Order**

• **Raising a Point:**

> o Any member may call the Chairperson’s attention to a point of order at any time, even while another member is speaking.

• **Scope and Limitation:**

> o The member must confine the intervention strictly to stating the point of order.
>
> o No speech or elaboration on the point is permitted.

• **Purpose:**

> o To ensure procedural correctness and maintain meeting orderly conduct of the

**4.24. Motion to be Put to Vote**

• Once a debate on a motion concludes, or if no debate occurs:

> o The Chairperson shall formally put the motion to vote.

• **This step ensures:**

> o Democratic decision-making through consensus or majority.
>
> o Closure of discussion on the proposed resolution.

**4.25. Decision on Questions**

• All matters in Academic Council meetings shall be resolved by majority vote of members present and voting.

• The Chairperson is entitled to vote on every question.

• In the event of a tie, the Chairperson shall exercise a casting vote to break the deadlock.

**4.26. Manner of Taking Vote/Poll**

• When any motion is put to vote, the Chairperson shall decide the method of voting.

• The discretion lies solely with the Chairperson to choose the voting procedure.

• Voting may be conducted through show of hands, voice vote, or ballot, as deemed appropriate by the Chairperson.

**4.27. Powers of the Chairperson**

• The Chairperson shall act as the sole authority on all points of order raised during the meeting.

• The Chairperson holds the power to call any member to order to maintain decorum.

• The Chairperson is empowered to enforce all decisions made on points of order without dispute.

• If the Chairperson stands up during the proceedings, any member who is speaking or intending to speak must immediately stop and sit down.

**4.28. Powers of Chairperson to Maintain Order**

• The Chairperson may initiate a special motion to address grossly disorderly conduct by any member.

• If the majority of members present approve the motion, the Chairperson can direct the concerned member to withdraw from the meeting.

• The member so directed must immediately comply and remain absent for the rest of the meeting on that day.

• This power is exercised to ensure the dignity and order of the meeting proceedings.

**4.29. Powers of Chairperson to Suspend or Adjourn Sittings**

• The Chairperson holds the authority to suspend or adjourn a meeting in case of grave disorder.

• The suspension or adjournment may be applied to the entire meeting or for a specified duration, as determined by the Chairperson.

• This power ensures that the meeting maintains decorum and orderly conduct during its proceedings.

• The Chairperson's decision in such matters is final and binding on all members present.

**4.30. Minutes of the Academic Council Meetings**

• The Principal's Office (PO) is responsible for recording the minutes of all proceedings of each meeting of the Academic Council.

• The recorded minutes must be signed by the Chairperson of the meeting for authentication.

• The PO, acting under the direction of the principal, shall:

> o Prepare and send a copy of the duly attested minutes
>
> o Ensure this is done within two weeks of the meeting
>
> o Distribute the minutes to each member of the Academic Council

• The minutes serve as an official record of the decisions and discussions held during the meeting.

**4.31. Objection to Minutes**

• Any member present at the meeting may raise objections to the correctness of the recorded minutes.

• Such objections must be submitted within ten days from the date the minutes were sent.

• **If no objections are raised within this period:**

> o The minutes are deemed to be correct.
>
> o They are considered as confirmed by all members.

• This rule ensures timely validation and avoids indefinite delays in confirming proceedings.

**4.32. Procedure to Decide the Objections**

1\. **Step 1:**

> o If any objection is received within the stipulated 10-day period, it must be:

• In writing,

• Addressed to the Principal Office,

• Clearly specify the corrections proposed in the minutes.

2\. **Step 2:**

> o The principal shall review and make the final decision on the validity and need for corrections.

3\. Step 3 (Optional Discretionary Action):

> o If the objection concerns an important or actionable matter, the Principal may:

• Seek clarification from the objecting member and others present at the meeting.

• Do so through direct communication or by circulation.

• Take a decision based on the collective feedback and context.

**4.33. Admission of Press and Visitors**

• Press and visitors are not automatically allowed to attend Academic Council meetings.

• Prior permission must be obtained from the principal for their admission.

• The principal holds full discretion to approve or deny access.

• This ensures confidentiality and maintains the order and decorum of the proceedings.

**5. Board of Studies (BoS)**

**5.1. Introduction**

The Board of Studies (BoS) is the principal academic body responsible for ensuring the quality and integrity of all programmes of study offered by a department. It oversees the entire academic experience, including teaching, learning, and assessment processes. A single BoS may govern more than one programme, depending on the structure and scope of the department. The Board is tasked with maintaining academic standards through the formulation and revision of curricula, ensuring that the content, structure, and delivery of courses align with institutional objectives and industry relevance. Ultimately, the BoS ensures that academic programmes meet the required benchmarks for quality assurance and contribute meaningfully to the educational mission of the institution.

**Definition and Purpose**

• A statutory academic body under each school or department

• Responsible for framing, revising, updating, and maintaining the quality of curriculum and academic content

• Acts as the first level of academic approval before Academic Council review

**Key Functions of BoS**

• Design and revise course syllabi for all programs under its scope

• Recommend new programs, specializations, and short-term certificate courses

• Approve evaluation patterns, teaching methodologies, and credit structures

• Suggest industry-relevant enhancements in the curriculum

• Ensure academic quality and benchmarking with national/international standards

• Recommend textbooks, reference books, e-resources, and lab manuals

• Propose examination patterns, internal assessment methods, and course outcomes

• Suggest measures for faculty development and innovation in pedagogy

• Facilitate feedback incorporation from students, alumni, industry, and peers

**Composition**

• **Chairperson:** Dean of the School (Ex-officio)

• **Members:**

> o Faculty of the concerned specialization (Ex-officio)
>
> o Two subject experts (nominated by Academic Council)
>
> o One expert from VC's panel of 6
>
> o Industry/corporate/alumni representative
>
> o Meritorious PG alumnus (nominated by Dean)
>
> o Co-opted external/internal experts as needed (VC approval)

**Meetings and Workflow**

• Convenes at least once a year (or as needed)

• Proposals finalized by BoS are forwarded to the Academic Council for final approval

• Maintains minutes of meetings, revisions, and member contributions

• Periodically reviews and upgrades curriculum based on technological and industrial trends

**Reporting Hierarchy**

• BoS → Academic Council → Board of Management

• Recommendations passed by BoS are subject to review by Academic Council and BOM

**5.2. Key Functions of the Board of Studies (BoS)**

1\. Curriculum Design and Development

• Formulate and update syllabi for all courses under its jurisdiction

• Introduce new programs (UG, PG, Diploma, Certificate) as per emerging trends

• Ensure alignment with Outcome-Based Education (OBE) and Bloom's Taxonomy

• Integrate interdisciplinary and multidisciplinary elements into the curriculum

• Promote inclusion of experiential learning, internships, and project-based modules

2\. Academic Quality Assurance

• Benchmark curriculum with leading national and international institutions

• Periodically review and revise courses to maintain academic relevance

• Ensure inclusion of latest advancements, technologies, and research findings

• Maintain coherence, balance, and progression across semesters

• Recommend best practices for instructional methods and academic delivery

3\. Faculty Development and Teaching Methods

• Recommend innovative and modern teaching pedagogies (e.g., flipped classroom, MOOCs)

• Support faculty in curriculum design, assessment methods, and skill-based education

• Encourage interdisciplinary teaching and guest lectures from academia/industry

• Propose faculty training, orientation, and refresher programs related to curriculum

4\. Assessment and Evaluation Framework

• Define the scheme of internal and external assessments (e.g., 60:40, 70:30)

• Propose examination patterns – theory, practicals, continuous assessment

• Recommend evaluation rubrics, grading criteria, and moderation mechanisms

• Standardize project, thesis, viva-voce, and lab evaluations across courses

5\. Academic Resource Recommendations

• Suggest updated textbooks, reference books, and online learning materials

• Recommend lab manuals, software tools, and ICT resources to support learning

• Propose procurement of journals, e-resources, and databases for library use

6\. Industry and Employability Alignment

• Collaborate with industry representatives to align programs with job market demands

• Suggest industry-based case studies, real-world problem statements, and capstone projects

• Integrate skill-based components, certifications, and employability modules

• Recommend domain-specific electives and technical workshops for students

7\. Feedback and Improvement Integration

• **Analyze feedback from:**

> o Students (course delivery, relevance)
>
> o Alumni (employability, industry alignment)
>
> o Employers (job-readiness, skill gaps)
>
> o Faculty (teaching challenges, improvements)

• Propose actionable changes based on analysis to continuously improve curriculum

8\. Approval and Documentation

• Approve syllabi, course files, lesson plans, and evaluation blueprints

• Maintain records of all curriculum changes, rationales, and expert opinions

• Submit recommendations to the Academic Council for ratification

**9. Types of Courses/Activities Constituting the Programmes of Study**

The academic programmes of study shall include the following structured course/activity types, each with defined engagement requirements:

**9.1. Lecture Courses**

**Definition**

• Courses involving structured lectures delivered by experts or qualified personnel in a particular field of learning, vocation, or professional practice.

**Key Characteristics**

• Focused on delivering theoretical knowledge and conceptual frameworks.

• Delivered by faculty, visiting professors, or industry professionals.

• May involve chalk-and-talk, multimedia presentations, or flipped classroom formats.

**Learning Components**

• Core instruction through lectures and classroom interaction.

• Supplemented with tutorials, readings, assignments, and tests.

• Designed to develop analytical thinking, domain knowledge, and foundational understanding.

**Assessment Methods**

• Mid-semester and end-semester examinations.

• Continuous Internal Evaluation (CIE) including quizzes, assignments, and class participation.

**Credit Allocation**

• Credits assigned based on number of instructional hours per week, typically:

> o 1 credit = 1 hour of lecture per week over a semester.

**9.2. Tutorial Courses**

Definition

• Courses that involve guided problem-solving, discussions, and analytical exercises conducted by qualified personnel in a specific field, vocation, or professional practice.

Key Characteristics

• Aim to reinforce concepts taught in lecture sessions.

• Encourage interactive learning, peer discussions, and individual engagement.

• Conducted in smaller groups to allow personalized attention and doubt clarification.

Learning Components

• Focused on application of theory, derivations, numerical problem-solving, and case-based discussions.

• Supports development of logical reasoning, critical thinking, and conceptual clarity.

Assessment Methods

• Graded assignments, quizzes, and participation.

• May include in-class presentations, written solutions, and tutorial logs.

Credit Allocation

• **Typically:**

> o 1 credit = 1 hour of tutorial per week over a semester.

• Often accompanies lecture courses as an integrated component.

**9.3. Practicum or Laboratory Work**

**Definition**

• A hands-on course requiring students to engage in projects, practical tasks, or laboratory activities to apply previously learned theoretical concepts under expert supervision.

**Key Characteristics**

• Designed to provide experiential learning in a controlled or real-world setting.

• Supervised by faculty members, lab instructors, or industry professionals.

• Reinforces technical proficiency, operational skills, and safety protocols relevant to the discipline.

**Learning Components**

• Experiments, design tasks, fabrication, coding, simulation, or measurement activities.

• Emphasis on data collection, analysis, documentation, and interpretation.

• Pre-lab and post-lab discussions to connect theory with practical outcomes.

**Assessment Methods**

• Continuous evaluation through lab reports, observations, practical exams, and viva voce.

• May include team projects, prototypes, or field-based deliverables.

**Credit Allocation**

• **Typically:**

> o 1 credit = 2 hours of practical/lab work per week over a semester.

• May be offered independently or as part of an integrated theory–lab course.

**9.4. Seminar**

**Definition**

• A course structured around interactive discussions, debates, or conversations focused on specific topics, readings, events, or experiences, guided by an expert or qualified personnel.

**Key Characteristics**

• Encourages active student participation and critical engagement with subject matter.

• Typically conducted in small groups to facilitate deeper discourse.

• Led by faculty or professionals with expertise in the relevant field.

**Learning Components**

• Analysis of assigned readings, case studies, or thematic issues.

• Student-led presentations, group discussions, role-play, or panel debates.

• Emphasis on developing communication, argumentation, and collaborative inquiry skills.

**Assessment Methods**

• Based on presentation quality, discussion contribution, reflective writing, and participation.

• May include summary reports, critique papers, or peer evaluations.

**Credit Allocation**

• **Typically:**

> o 1 credit = 1–2 hours of seminar participation per week over a semester.

• Often included as part of advanced coursework or final-year curriculum.

**9.5. Internship**

**Definition**

• A course designed to engage students in professional activities, work experiences, or cooperative education programs with external entities, offering real-world exposure under the supervision of industry or domain experts.

**Key Characteristics**

• Focuses on induction into actual work environments and practices.

• Conducted with local industries, government/private organizations, businesses, artists, or crafts persons.

• Aims to bridge the gap between academic learning and practical application.

**Learning Components**

• Exposure to organizational workflows, technical tools, workplace culture, and team collaboration.

• Students undertake defined tasks, projects, or roles aligned with their field of study.

• Encourages the development of professional competencies, ethics, and problem-solving skills.

**Assessment Methods**

• Based on internship reports, attendance, feedback from the supervisor, and oral presentations/viva.

• May include a learning journal, performance appraisal, and self-reflection.

**Credit Allocation**

• **Typically:**

> o 2–4 credits depending on the duration (e.g., 4–8 weeks = 2 credits, 8–12 weeks = 3–4 credits).

• Mandatory in many professional programs and often taken during summer/winter breaks or in the final year.

**9.6. Studio Activities**

**Definition**

• Studio activities are experiential learning modules where students engage in creative or artistic tasks to produce a specific visual, design, or aesthetic outcome.

**Key Characteristics**

• Centered on individual or group artistic expression and creation.

• Emphasizes visual design, spatial understanding, aesthetics, and creativity.

• Common in fields such as architecture, design, fine arts, animation, multimedia, etc.

**Learning Components**

• Students perform tasks such as drawing, modeling, designing, sculpting, painting, digital editing, etc.

• Activities are conducted in a studio/lab-like environment, often under faculty or artist supervision.

• Includes idea generation, prototype development, critique sessions, and portfolio creation.

**Assessment Methods**

• Evaluation based on originality, execution quality, creative process, and final output.

• Includes critiques (peer/faculty), documentation, and display or exhibition of work.

**Credit Allocation**

• **Typically:**

> o 1 credit = 2–3 hours of studio activity per week over a semester.

• May span individual projects, collaborative workshops, or thematic explorations.

**9.7. Field Practice / Projects**

**Definition**

• Courses that involve students in field-based learning or real-world projects, typically conducted outside the classroom, under the supervision of domain experts or external professionals.

**Key Characteristics**

• Immersive experiences focused on practical exposure, data collection, community engagement, or problem-solving in situ.

• Enables application of academic knowledge to real-life environments.

• Often used in disciplines such as agriculture, civil engineering, environmental science, social work, public health, etc.

**Learning Components**

• Activities include field surveys, site analysis, case studies, community projects, ecological observations, etc.

• Emphasizes development of research skills, observational accuracy, teamwork, and contextual understanding.

• May include interaction with communities, stakeholders, or local authorities.

**Assessment Methods**

• Based on field reports, presentations, documentation, supervisor feedback, and reflective journals.

• May include viva voce or group evaluation sessions.

**Credit Allocation**

• **Typically:**

> o 1 credit = 2–3 hours of field engagement per week, or equivalent block fieldwork hours.

• Duration and depth vary depending on project complexity and course objectives.

**9.8. Community Engagement and Service**

**Definition**

• Courses that engage students in field-based projects or activities aimed at addressing real-world socio-economic issues, under the guidance of experts or community leaders.

**Key Characteristics**

• Focused on social responsibility, civic engagement, and practical problem-solving.

• Bridges the gap between classroom learning and community realities.

• Conducted in collaboration with NGOs, local bodies, rural or urban communities, etc.

**Learning Components**

• Activities may include awareness campaigns, health surveys, literacy drives, environmental clean-up, infrastructure assessments, etc.

• Encourages collaboration, empathy, ethical reasoning, and cultural sensitivity.

• Students are expected to identify issues, propose interventions, and reflect on their experiences.

**Assessment Methods**

• Evaluation through field reports, reflection papers, community feedback, and faculty assessment.

• May include presentations, group discussions, and documentation of impact.

**Credit Allocation**

• **Typically:**

> o 1–2 credits based on hours of fieldwork (e.g., 30–60 hours of community work).

• Can be a standalone module or integrated within broader social innovation or extension programs.

**9.9. Skill Enhancement Courses**

**Definition**

• Courses focused on developing practical, professional, and interpersonal skills to improve students’ employability and job-readiness.

**Key Characteristics**

• Oriented towards hands-on training, soft skills development, and industry-relevant competencies.

• Designed to align with current market demands and student career aspirations.

• Flexible in structure to accommodate institutional strengths and local opportunities.

**Learning Components**

• **May include modules on:**

> o Communication skills, presentation skills, resume writing, group discussions
>
> o Technical tools/software, digital literacy, programming, data handling
>
> o Entrepreneurship, design thinking, financial literacy, leadership training

**Assessment Methods**

• Continuous evaluation through practical demonstrations, assignments, presentations, and performance assessments.

• May include external certification or industry-assessed tasks.

**Credit Allocation**

• **Typically:**

> o 2–3 credits per course, depending on content depth and contact hours.

**12. Grading and Marking Policy**

Grading and marking are essential components of the assessment process in higher education, determining how students' work is evaluated, reported, and ultimately used to award final grades. This policy ensures that grading is fair, transparent, and consistent, and that it aligns with the course's learning objectives and outcomes.

**12.1. Purpose of Grading and Marking**

• **Evaluate Student Performance:** Grading and marking provide a clear and objective measure of students' understanding and application of course material.

• **Certify Achievement:** Grades serve as a formal certification of a student’s academic achievement, indicating their level of proficiency in the subject.

• **Encourage Improvement:** The grading process provides students with feedback on their strengths and weaknesses, guiding them toward areas for improvement.

• **Ensure Fairness and Equity:** A well-established grading system ensures equitable treatment of all students and a consistent basis for evaluating performance.

**12.2. Grading System**

1\. **Grading Scale:**

> o **Letter Grades:** Institutions typically use a letter grade scale with corresponding grade points. Commonly used letter grades include:
>
> o A (Excellent)
>
> o B (Good)
>
> o C (Satisfactory)
>
> o D (Pass)
>
> o F (Fail)
>
> o **Grade Points:** Each letter grade is associated with a numerical value (e.g., GPA scale). For example:
>
> o A = 4.0
>
> o B = 3.0
>
> o C = 2.0
>
> o D = 1.0
>
> o F = 0.0

2\. **Percentage Scale:**

> o In some institutions, percentage grading is used, where the total score for each student is expressed as a percentage out of 100.

3\. **Grade Conversion:**

> o Institutions may convert percentage scores into letter grades or GPA based on predefined criteria, for example:
>
> o 90% – 100% = A (Excellent)
>
> o 80% – 89% = B (Good)
>
> o 70% – 79% = C (Satisfactory)
>
> o 60% – 69% = D (Pass)
>
> o Below 60% = F (Fail)

4\. **Plus and Minus Grades:**

> o Some grading systems also use plus (+) and minus (-) modifiers to add precision to the evaluation, e.g., B+ (3.3), C- (1.7), etc.

**12.3. Marking Scheme**

1\. **Clear and Detailed Rubrics:**

> o Marking rubrics should be provided for all assignments, exams, projects, and other graded components. Rubrics outline:

• The criteria for evaluation (e.g., clarity, accuracy, creativity, etc.).

• Weightage of each criterion.

• Descriptive performance levels (e.g., Excellent, Good, Needs Improvement).

> o Rubrics ensure that the grading process is transparent and that students understand how their work will be evaluated.

2\. **Weightage for Evaluation Components:**

> o Each assessment component (e.g., assignments, quizzes, exams, projects) should have a clearly defined weightage.
>
> o **Example of weight distribution:**

• **Assignments:** 20%

• **Quizzes:** 10%

**13. SGPA (Semester Grade Point Average)**

The Semester Grade Point Average (SGPA) is a critical metric used in higher education to measure the academic performance of students in a particular semester. It is calculated based on the grades obtained in all courses during that semester and provides a snapshot of a student's overall performance. SGPA is used by many academic institutions as a tool for determining academic standing and eligibility for future courses or graduation.

**13.1. Definition of SGPA**

• SGPA is the weighted average of the grade points obtained by a student in all the courses during a particular semester.

• It provides an indication of a student’s overall academic performance for that specific semester.

• SGPA is used to evaluate students' success and to assess if they meet the minimum requirements for graduation or progression to the next level of study.

**13.2. Formula for SGPA Calculation**

**The formula for calculating SGPA is:**

**SGPA = Σ(Ci × Gi) / ΣCi (summed over i = 1 to n)**

**Where:**

• Ci = Credit for the iᵗʰ course

• Gi = Grade point obtained in the iᵗʰ course

• n = Total number of courses in the semester

**13.3. Step-by-Step Calculation of SGPA**

1\. **List All Courses:** Identify all the courses taken in the semester and their corresponding credits.

2\. **Assign Grade Points:** Convert the grades received in each course into grade points using the institution’s grading scale. For example:

> o A = 4.0
>
> o B = 3.0
>
> o C = 2.0
>
> o D = 1.0
>
> o F = 0.0 (Fail)

3\. **Multiply Grade Points by Course Credits:** For each course, multiply the grade points by the number of credits assigned to the course.

4\. **Sum of Weighted Grade Points:** Add all the weighted grade points (credit × grade point) for all the courses.

5\. **Sum of Course Credits:** Add the total number of credits for all the courses in the semester.

6\. **Divide:** Divide the sum of weighted grade points by the total number of credits to obtain the SGPA.

**13.4. Example of SGPA Calculation**

Let’s say a student has taken the following courses in a semester:

| **Course** | **Credits (C)** | **Grade Obtained** | **Grade Points (G)** | **Credit × Grade Points** |
|------------|-----------------|--------------------|----------------------|---------------------------|
| Course 1   | 3               | A                  | 4.0                  | 12.0                      |
| Course 2   | 4               | B                  | 3.0                  | 12.0                      |
| Course 3   | 3               | C                  | 2.0                  | 6.0                       |
| Course 4   | 2               | A                  | 4.0                  | 8.0                       |

• Sum of Weighted Grade Points = 12.0 + 12.0 + 6.0 + 8.0 = 38.0

• Total Credits = 3 + 4 + 3 + 2 = 12

Now, applying the SGPA formula:

**SGPA = 38.0 / 12 = 3.17**

The SGPA for this semester is 3.17.

SGPA is often mapped to a letter grade scale to give students an understanding of their performance relative to the institution’s grading criteria:

| **SGPA Range** | **Letter Grade** |
|----------------|------------------|
| 3.75 - 4.00    | A+               |
| 3.50 - 3.74    | A                |
| 3.00 - 3.49    | B+               |
| 2.50 - 2.99    | B                |
| 2.00 - 2.49    | C                |
| Below 2.00     | F                |

**13.6. Importance of SGPA**

**13.7. Challenges and Considerations**

1\. **SGPA vs. Cumulative GPA:**

2\. **Weight of SGPA in Degree Classification:**

> o Institutions may use both SGPA and CGPA in determining a student’s final academic standing. Some institutions give more importance to CGPA than individual SGPA in determining final classification.

**14. CGPA (Cumulative Grade Point Average)**

The Cumulative Grade Point Average (CGPA) is a measure of a student's academic performance over the entire duration of their degree program. It is calculated by considering all semesters’ Grade Point Averages (SGPA), allowing both students and instructors to assess overall academic progress. CGPA provides a comprehensive view of a student’s performance across all courses throughout the academic program, rather than on a semester-by-semester basis like SGPA.

**14.1. Definition of CGPA**

• CGPA is the weighted average of grade points obtained by a student across all semesters.

• Unlike SGPA, which is calculated for individual semesters, CGPA aggregates all the SGPAs of the student's academic career.

• CGPA is typically used for determining final academic standing and eligibility for graduation, scholarships, and job placements.

**14.2. Formula for CGPA Calculation**

**The formula to calculate CGPA is:**

**CGPA = Σ(Ci × Gi) / ΣCi (summed over i = 1 to n)**

**Where:**

• Ci = Credit for the iᵗʰ course

• Gi = Grade point obtained in the iᵗʰ course

• n = Total number of courses taken across all semesters

**14.3. Step-by-Step Calculation of CGPA:**

1\. **List All Courses Across Semesters:**

Identify all the courses taken during the entire academic program, including courses from each semester.

2\. **Assign Grade Points:**

Convert the grades received in each course into grade points according to the institution's grading scale (e.g., A = 4.0, B = 3.0, etc.).

3\. **Multiply Grade Points by Course Credits:**

For each course, multiply the grade points obtained by the number of credits assigned to the course.

4\. **Sum of Weighted Grade Points:**

Add all the weighted grade points (credit × grade point) across all semesters.

5\. **Sum of Course Credits:**

Add up all the credits from all courses taken over the entire degree program.

6\. **Divide:**

Divide the sum of weighted grade points by the total number of credits to get the CGPA.

**14.4. Example of CGPA Calculation**

Let’s assume a student has completed the following courses across different semesters:

| **Course** | **Credits (C)** | **Grade Obtained** | **Grade Points (G)** | **Credit × Grade Points** |
|------------|-----------------|--------------------|----------------------|---------------------------|
| Course 1   | 3               | A                  | 4.0                  | 12.0                      |
| Course 2   | 4               | B                  | 3.0                  | 12.0                      |
| Course 3   | 3               | C                  | 2.0                  | 6.0                       |
| Course 4   | 2               | A                  | 4.0                  | 8.0                       |
| Course 5   | 3               | B+                 | 3.3                  | 9.9                       |

• Sum of Weighted Grade Points = 12.0 + 12.0 + 6.0 + 8.0 + 9.9 = 47.9

• Total Credits = 3 + 4 + 3 + 2 + 3 = 15

Now, applying the CGPA formula:

**CGPA = 47.9 / 15 = 3.19**

The CGPA for this student is 3.19.

**15. The Pass/Fail System**

The Pass/Fail system is a grading policy used by some educational institutions to assess students' performance based on whether they meet the required minimum criteria for a course. This system provides an alternative to the traditional letter-grade system, offering a less stressful environment where students focus on mastering the material rather than obsessing over achieving a specific letter grade.

**15.1. Purpose of the Pass/Fail System**

• **Reduces Pressure:** The system is designed to reduce the academic pressure on students, especially for courses that are outside their major or area of specialization.

• **Encourages Learning:** By focusing on whether students have mastered the material, it encourages them to focus on understanding rather than competing for higher grades.

• **Promotes Risk-Taking:** The Pass/Fail system allows students to take courses in subjects they might otherwise avoid due to fear of failure, promoting a more holistic education.

• **Fairness:** It ensures that students who meet the basic requirements for a course are acknowledged without the pressure of letter grades that could affect their GPA or academic record.

**15.2. Structure of the Pass/Fail System**

1\. **Pass Criteria:**

> o Pass typically means that a student has met the minimum standards for the course, demonstrating a basic level of understanding and competency in the subject matter.
>
> o **Minimum Marks:** The pass grade is generally defined by a minimum percentage (e.g., 50%, 60%, or another threshold) or a grade point (e.g., 2.0 GPA or equivalent grade point).

2\. **Fail Criteria:**

> o Fail means that the student has not met the minimum requirements for the course.
>
> o If a student fails, they are generally required to retake the course or make up for the missing requirements in another way (e.g., supplementary exams or extra assignments).

3\. **Grades and Documentation:**

> o In the Pass/Fail system, the student’s academic transcript will show Pass (P) or Fail (F) for the course, rather than a letter grade (A, B, C, etc.).
>
> o **The Pass/Fail system does not impact the GPA:** Since there are no grade points assigned to Pass/Fail courses, they generally do not contribute to the calculation of the student's GPA.
>
> o **Elective and Core Courses:** Pass/Fail may apply to elective courses or specific courses where performance is not the central focus. It is important to check with the institution whether core courses or major-specific courses are eligible for the Pass/Fail system.

**15.3. Benefits of the Pass/Fail System**

1\. **Reduces Stress:**

> o The Pass/Fail system is less stressful because students don’t have to worry about achieving a particular grade to maintain their GPA. This system can help alleviate anxiety, especially in subjects outside their main area of interest.

2\. **Encourages Exploration:**

> o It encourages students to explore new subjects or areas of learning without the fear of failing. For example, students may take a course in the humanities, arts, or other fields outside their core discipline.

3\. **Fosters Mastery Over Grades:**

> o The system shifts the focus from competing for the highest grades to simply
>
> o meeting the course’s learning objectives. This helps students focus more on mastering the material than on earning a high grade.

4\. **Flexibility:**

> o The Pass/Fail system offers flexibility for students who may be struggling with personal or academic challenges, allowing them to pass courses without the added pressure of obtaining specific letter grades.

**15.4. Challenges of the Pass/Fail System**

1\. **Lack of Detailed Feedback:**

> o Since the system only indicates whether a student has passed or failed, it does not provide detailed feedback or recognition of academic excellence. Students who perform well may not get the recognition they deserve.

2\. **Reduced Motivation for High Achievement:**

> o The absence of letter grades can sometimes result in lower motivation for high achievers. Some students may not feel the incentive to perform at their best in Pass/Fail courses, especially when they are only focused on meeting the basic requirements.

3\. **Potential for Grade Inflation:**

> o There is a risk that students may underperform and still pass, potentially leading to a lack of rigor in the assessment. This could undermine the value of the course and the degree itself.

4\. **Limited Application:**

> o Not all courses are eligible for the Pass/Fail system, and it may not be suitable for core or major-required courses. Students might not be able to use the Pass/Fail system for courses that contribute to their overall academic standing or progression.

5\. **Limited Employer Insight:**

> o Employers or graduate schools may prefer traditional grades to evaluate academic performance. The Pass/Fail designation doesn’t offer enough information about how well a student understood the material or their academic rigor.

**15.5. Implementation Guidelines**

1\. **Eligibility for Pass/Fail:**

> o The institution should clearly define which courses are eligible for the Pass/Fail grading system, typically electives or non-core courses.
>
> o Students must usually opt-in to take a course on a Pass/Fail basis, and this decision should be made at the beginning of the semester.

2\. **Minimum Performance Standards:**

> o A clear threshold for passing (e.g., 50%, 60%) should be defined. The course syllabus should explicitly state the minimum performance requirement for passing.

3\. **Limit on Pass/Fail Courses:**

> o Many institutions impose a limit on the number of courses that can be taken under the Pass/Fail option. For example, students may be allowed to take only a certain percentage of their total courses on a Pass/Fail basis.

4\. **Deadlines for Choosing Pass/Fail:**

> o Students should be given a specific deadline by which they can switch from the traditional grading system to the Pass/Fail option. This decision should be made with academic advising and should consider the student's academic standing.

5\. **Impact on GPA:**

> o Students should be informed that Pass/Fail courses will not affect their GPA. However, a failing grade in a Pass/Fail course may negatively affect their academic standing or progression.

**15.6. Rationale for Pass/Fail System**

• **Encourages Exploration and Learning:** The Pass/Fail system provides an opportunity for students to take risks by exploring new subjects without the fear of impacting their GPA.

• **Reduces Pressure:** This system reduces the anxiety of students who may perform poorly in certain courses but are still capable of excelling in others.

• **Supports Holistic Development:** By removing the emphasis on grades, the Pass/Fail system encourages students to focus on mastering content and developing skills, fostering a more holistic approach to learning.

This Pass/Fail Policy ensures that students can focus on learning and mastery of subjects without the constant pressure of competing for top grades. It is especially useful for courses outside the core curriculum and helps maintain a balanced academic workload for students.

• **Reduce Pressure on Students:** The CR/NC system allows students to explore subjects outside their major or area of expertise without the fear of impacting their GPA.

• **Encourage Risk-Taking:** By removing the pressure of achieving a letter grade, the CR/NC system encourages students to take elective courses or subjects in which they may not feel confident.

• **Focus on Learning:** The primary goal is for students to focus on learning the material without stressing over the grade they will receive, particularly in non-core courses.

• **Promote Exploration:** It provides students with the opportunity to explore interdisciplinary courses, pursue academic interests outside their major, or take passion projects without the fear of failure.

**17.2. How the Credit/No Credit System Works**

1\. **Grade Assignment:**

> o Credit (CR): Indicates that the student has completed the course successfully, meeting the minimum performance requirements, typically determined by the instructor or department. A grade of "Credit" does not impact the student’s GPA.
>
> o No Credit (NC): Indicates that the student has not met the minimum requirements of the course and therefore does not receive credit for it. A grade of "No Credit" also does not affect the GPA but may have an impact on academic standing or progression.

2\. **Eligibility for CR:**

> o In most cases, students must earn a minimum percentage or grade point to receive Credit for the course. For example, students may need to achieve a C or above to receive CR in a course.
>
> o The minimum requirement for No Credit might vary by institution, but typically, students who fail to meet the baseline performance standards are assigned No Credit.

3\. **Impact on GPA:**

> o Credit (CR) courses do not affect the student’s Grade Point Average (GPA), as the system does not assign grade points.
>
> o No Credit (NC) courses also have no impact on the GPA, but they may appear on the student’s transcript, indicating a failed attempt in the course.
>
> o The CR/NC system ensures that students can take courses without worrying about grades impacting their GPA.

4\. **Availability:**

> o The CR/NC system is often available for elective courses or non-major courses, allowing students to broaden their academic experience without the pressure of high academic stakes.
>
> o Some institutions offer a limited number of courses in the CR/NC system, while others allow students to choose certain courses on a CR/NC basis.

**17.3. Benefits of the Credit/No Credit System**

1\. **Exploration of Non-Core Subjects:**

> o The CR/NC system encourages students to explore subjects outside their major or area of expertise, without worrying about grades or GPA consequences. This is particularly helpful in interdisciplinary or elective courses.

2\. **Reduces Academic Stress:**

> o Since grades are not awarded in the traditional sense, students experience less academic stress. This creates a healthier academic environment where the focus is on learning rather than competing for grades.

3\. **Fosters Lifelong Learning:**

> o The system promotes a growth mindset, where students can focus on gaining knowledge and experience rather than fixating on obtaining specific letter grades. This aligns with the goal of lifelong learning and personal development.

4\. **Encourages Risk-Taking and Innovation:**

> o Students are more likely to take intellectual risks, try innovative ideas, and enroll in courses outside their comfort zone. This helps students diversify their knowledge base and grow in new academic directions.

5\. **Improved Focus on Mastery:**

> o Without the distraction of grades, students can focus more on mastering the subject. This allows for deeper learning and better retention of knowledge.

**17.4. Disadvantages of the Credit/No Credit System**

1\. **Lack of Detailed Performance Information:**

> o The CR/NC system does not provide a detailed indication of how well a student has performed in the course. For instance, a student who barely passes may receive the same “Credit” designation as a student who excels, which may mask performance differences.

2\. **Limited Use for Core Courses:**

> o The CR/NC system is typically limited to elective courses or courses that are not critical to the student’s core degree program. This may not always be suitable for courses that require precise performance evaluation, such as major-specific or graduate-level courses.

3\. **Impact on Post-Graduate or Employer Expectations:**

> o Some employers or graduate programs may require specific grades or GPA thresholds. The CR/NC system does not provide enough information about students’ specific achievements in a course, which may disadvantage students applying for competitive positions or graduate programs.

4\. **Perceived Lack of Rigor:**

> o Some students may feel that courses taken under the CR/NC system lack academic rigor, which could lead to them taking courses that they may not otherwise attempt if they knew grades would be awarded.

5\. **Missed Opportunities for Recognition:**

> o In the CR/NC system, students who excel in non-core courses may miss out on the opportunity to have their achievements recognised through letter grades or GPA, which might otherwise enhance their transcript and academic profile.

**17.5. Guidelines for Implementing Credit/No Credit System**

1\. **Clear Criteria for CR/NC:**

> o Institutions must provide clear criteria for determining what constitutes Credit or No Credit. This typically involves a minimum grade or percentage that must be earned to receive credit.

2\. **Limit the Number of CR/NC Courses:**

> o Most institutions limit the number of courses that can be taken on a CR/NC basis to prevent students from over-relying on this option for core courses.

3\. **Transparency in Transcripts:**

> o Transcripts should clearly indicate which courses were taken on a CR/NC basis. While grades are not recorded, the transcript should show whether the course was completed with Credit or failed (No Credit).

4\. **Deadline for CR/NC Option:**

> o Students should be allowed to opt for CR/NC grading at the beginning of the course or by a specified deadline. This decision should be made before receiving any grades in the course to maintain fairness.

5\. **Advising and Counseling:**

> o Academic advisors should be available to counsel students on the appropriate use of the CR/NC option, ensuring that students fully understand the implications for their academic progress and career prospects.

**17.6. Rationale for the Credit/No Credit System**

• **Flexibility in Learning:** The CR/NC system allows students to take academic risks, explore subjects outside their major, and focus on learning without the fear of affecting their GPA.

• **Academic Freedom:** It promotes intellectual freedom and curiosity by encouraging students to engage in a wide variety of courses without the pressure of grade-based evaluation.

• **Student Well-Being:** The system helps reduce the stress and pressure often associated with letter grades, allowing students to learn at their own pace and focus on mastery of the content.

This Credit/No Credit (CR/NC) Policy encourages a balanced and stress-free academic environment, where students can focus on learning, exploration, and personal development. By reducing the pressure of traditional grading, it enhances the educational experience, promotes risk-taking, and fosters intellectual curiosity.

**18. Relative Grading System**

The Relative Grading System is a grading model used by many academic institutions to assess students' performance relative to the performance of their peers in the same course. Unlike absolute grading, where students are graded based on predefined thresholds or fixed marks, relative grading assigns grades based on the distribution of marks among students, ensuring that students are compared to each other rather than an objective standard.

**18.1. Purpose of the Relative Grading System**

• **Normalize Grades Across Cohorts:** The primary purpose of relative grading is to ensure that grades are normalized in relation to the overall performance of the class, accounting for variations in exam difficulty and other external factors.

• **Reduce Grade Inflation:** It helps avoid grade inflation where a disproportionately high number of students may receive high grades in an easy exam, ensuring that grades reflect relative performance.

• **Fairness in Assessment:** By comparing students’ performance to that of their peers, relative grading aims to level the playing field, especially in courses where exams might be particularly difficult or content-heavy.

• **Motivation for Peer Comparison:** The system encourages healthy competition among students, motivating them to perform better by comparing their work to that of their peers.

**18.2. How the Relative Grading System Works**

1\. **Distribution of Scores:**

> o The grade boundaries are determined by where the students' scores fall in comparison to each other. For example, the top 20% of students might receive an A, the next 30% might receive a B, and so on.

2\. **Grade Distribution:**

> o **Common grade distributions in relative grading include:**

• Top 10-20%: Awarded A (Excellent)

• Next 30%: Awarded B (Good)

• Middle 40%: Awarded C (Satisfactory)

• Bottom 10-20%: Awarded D or F (Fail)

> o The exact percentage for each grade can vary depending on the instructor, institution, or course requirements.

3\. **Curving the Grades:**

> o Grading curve is applied based on how well the class performs as a whole. For example, if the average score of the class is low, the instructor might “curve” the grades so that more students receive higher grades, even if their raw scores are low.

4\. **Class Percentile:**

> o Percentile rankings of students are calculated based on the relative score in the course, and grades are assigned accordingly. For example, a student who ranks in the top 10% of the class might receive an A, while a student ranked in the bottom 10% might receive an F.

**18.3. Advantages of the Relative Grading System**

1\. **Fairness in Difficult Exams:**

> o In courses with very difficult exams, relative grading ensures that a student’s grade reflects how well they performed compared to the class rather than the difficulty of the exam.

2\. **Accountability:**

> o Relative grading promotes peer comparison and accountability. Students are aware that their grades depend not only on their own effort but also on how well their peers perform.

3\. **Encourages Motivation and Competition:**

> o It creates an environment of healthy competition, where students push themselves to perform better in comparison to their peers. This can lead to higher overall class performance.

4\. **Addresses Grade Inflation:**

> o Relative grading helps to counter grade inflation, which can occur when all students perform well in easy courses. It ensures that only a certain percentage of students can achieve the highest grade.

5\. **Standardized Grading:**

> o This system standardizes grading across different cohorts of students, especially when instructors vary in the difficulty of exams or assignment grading.

**18.4. Disadvantages of the Relative Grading System**

1\. **Competition Over Collaboration:**

> o The system might foster competition over collaboration, which can undermine teamwork and collective learning. Students might become less willing to help one another or engage in collaborative projects.

2\. **Stress and Anxiety:**

> o Relative grading can lead to heightened stress and anxiety among students, especially those who may perform well but are ranked lower compared to their peers.

3\. **Limited Recognition of Individual Effort:**

> o In relative grading, individual effort can sometimes be overlooked. For example, a student who does well but is in a particularly strong cohort might receive a lower grade despite putting in significant effort.

4\. **Unpredictable Grade Outcomes:**

> o Since the grading depends on how others perform, students cannot be sure what grade they will get based on their own performance. The outcome is not entirely in their control.

5\. **Risk of Unfair Distribution:**

> o If the class is not well-distributed in terms of performance, the system may lead to unfair grade distributions. For instance, if all students perform poorly, it could result in an excessive number of students receiving failing grades.

**18.5. Implementation Guidelines for the Relative Grading System**

1\. **Clear Communication of Expectations:**

> o Instructors must clearly communicate to students how their grades will be calculated based on relative performance. This includes explaining the grading curve and how it impacts final grades.

2\. **Transparency in Grading:**

> o Grading policies should be transparent, with a clear understanding of how grades are distributed among students. This helps manage student expectations and reduces confusion.

3\. **Statistical Analysis of Scores:**

> o Instructors should use statistical tools (e.g., mean, median, standard deviation) to distribute grades fairly, ensuring that the grade boundaries reflect the overall class performance.

4\. **Fair Curve Application:**

> o The curve should be applied in a way that is fair and balanced, considering the overall class performance and the difficulty level of the course. A curve that is too aggressive may hurt students who perform well but are ranked lower relative to their peers.

5\. **Limit the Use of Relative Grading:**

> o Relative grading should be limited to certain types of courses, particularly elective courses or courses with a large number of students. Core or major-required courses should be evaluated using absolute grading to avoid excessive reliance on peer performance.

**18.6. When to Use the Relative Grading System**

1\. **Large Classes:**

> o Relative grading is particularly effective in large classes where a broad distribution of student performance is expected. It helps standardize the grading process and ensures that students are evaluated based on their relative performance.

2\. **Competitive Courses:**

> o It is useful for courses with highly competitive content, where students may be vying for limited spots in advanced programs, scholarships, or job opportunities.

3\. **Courses with Variable Exam Difficulty:**

> o If exam difficulty can vary between semesters or instructors, relative grading helps ensure that all students are assessed according to their performance compared to their peers, rather than fixed score cutoffs.

**18.7. Rationale for Relative Grading System Policy**

• **Promotes Fair Evaluation:** The relative grading system ensures that students’ grades reflect their performance in comparison to the overall class, making it a fairer and more consistent way of grading in large or difficult courses.

• **Encourages Motivation:** By promoting peer comparison, students are encouraged to excel and push themselves beyond basic requirements, driving a culture of academic competition and excellence.

• **Addresses Variations in Course Difficulty:** The system compensates for variations in exam difficulty and instructor grading practices, ensuring that all students are evaluated based on relative standards.

The Relative Grading System encourages fairness, consistency, and healthy competition, while also helping students navigate courses where performance might be impacted by challenging exams or competitive cohorts. It remains an effective way to assess students when absolute grading may not be suitable or could lead to unfair evaluations.

**19. Normal Curve and Its Role in Grading Systems**

The Normal Curve, also known as the Gaussian distribution or Bell Curve, is a statistical concept that plays a significant role in grading systems, particularly in the context of relative grading. It helps in determining how grades are distributed across a class or population, ensuring that the grading process is fair and reflects natural variation in student performance.

**19.1. What is the Normal Curve?**

The Normal Curve is a probability distribution that represents how data points (such as student scores) are expected to be distributed around the mean (average). In a perfect normal distribution:

• Most data points (scores) are clustered around the mean, with fewer data points appearing as we move away from the mean.

• The distribution forms a symmetric, bell-shaped curve where:

> o 50% of data points lie below the mean.
>
> o 50% of data points lie above the mean.

**Key Characteristics:**

• Mean (μ): The average score of all the data points.

• Standard Deviation (σ): A measure of the spread or dispersion of scores. A smaller standard deviation means the data points are tightly packed around the mean, while a larger standard deviation indicates more spread out data.

**19.2. How the Normal Curve Relates to Grading**

In the context of grading, the normal curve can be used to distribute grades fairly based on how students perform relative to one another. This approach helps to ensure that grades reflect natural variations in student performance, preventing artificially high or low grade distributions that may occur due to variations in exam difficulty or instructor grading practices.

1\. Grade Distribution:

> o The normal curve can be used to distribute grades in a manner that reflects the relative performance of students. Typically, the distribution looks like this:

• Mean (μ): Represents the average score in the class.

• Standard Deviations (σ): Marks on the curve that divide the scores into specific groups. For example:

• ±1 standard deviation (σ) from the mean covers approximately 68% of the scores (from 34% below to 34% above the mean).

• ±2 standard deviations (2σ) cover approximately 95% of the scores.

• ±3 standard deviations (3σ) cover approximately 99.7% of the scores.

2\. Applying the Normal Curve to Grades:

> o In the Relative Grading System, instructors can apply the normal curve to determine how to distribute letter grades. The grades can be allocated as follows:

• A (Excellent): Given to students who are at or above 1 standard deviation above the mean (e.g., top 15-20% of students).

• B (Good): Given to students whose scores lie within 1 standard deviation above or below the mean (e.g., middle 50-60%).

• C (Satisfactory): Given to students below the average but not failing (e.g., 1 to 2 standard deviations below the mean).

• D (Pass) and F (Fail): Given to students who are more than 2 standard deviations below the mean.

3\. Example of Grade Distribution Based on Normal Curve:

> o Class Average (Mean = μ): 75%
>
> o Standard Deviation (σ): 10%
>
> o Using the normal curve, the grade distribution might look like this:

• A (90-100%): Top 10-15% of students, 1 or more standard deviations above the mean.

• B (80-89%): 34% of students, between the mean and 1 standard deviation above the mean.

• C (70-79%): 34% of students, around the mean.

• D (60-69%): 10-15% of students, 1 or more standard deviations below the mean.

• F (\<60%): Bottom 10% of students, more than 2 standard deviations below the mean.

**19.3. Advantages of Using the Normal Curve in Grading**

1\. Fair Distribution of Grades:

> o The normal curve helps in ensuring that grades are distributed in a fair manner across all students, considering natural variations in performance.

2\. Prevents Grade Inflation:

> o By adhering to the distribution set by the normal curve, instructors can prevent grade inflation where an unusually high number of students receive high grades, regardless of their actual performance.

3\. Aligns with Statistical Standards:

> o The normal curve aligns grading with statistical standards, ensuring that grades reflect natural variation in student abilities, which is typically expected in large, diverse classes.

4\. Reduces the Impact of Exam Difficulty:

> o If an exam is particularly difficult, the normal curve allows for a grading curve that can help adjust for lower scores, ensuring that students are graded relative to their peers rather than by arbitrary thresholds.

5\. Encourages Consistent Performance:

> o Students are encouraged to perform relative to their peers, helping to create a culture of academic rigor where excellence is measured against the entire class.

**19.4. Disadvantages of Using the Normal Curve in Grading**

1\. Not Suitable for Small Classes:

> o The normal curve works best in larger classes where there is a wide spread of student scores. In small classes, the distribution may not reflect a normal curve, and applying this model may lead to unfair grade allocations.

2\. Disadvantages for High-Achieving Students:

> o In some cases, if the majority of students perform well, a significant number of high-achieving students might not receive the top grades, as the top grades are reserved for the few highest performers in the class.

3\. Potential for Unintended Consequences:

> o The normal curve may lead to unnecessary competition and stress among students, particularly if they are overly focused on being in the top percentage of the class. It can create a focus on ranking rather than mastering the content.

4\. Exclusion of Mastery-Based Grading:

> o The relative grading system based on the normal curve does not always measure absolute mastery of the subject matter. A student who has mastered the material but is in a competitive cohort might receive a lower grade than expected, even if they have demonstrated understanding.

**19.5. Implementing the Normal Curve in Grading**

1\. Set Grade Boundaries Based on Class Performance:

> o The mean and standard deviation of the class’s performance should be calculated. This statistical data should be used to determine grade boundaries (A, B, C, etc.) relative to the class’s performance.

2\. Adjust for Outliers:

> o If there are outliers in the class (e.g., unusually high or low performers), instructors may choose to adjust the curve to ensure that the distribution of grades remains reasonable and fair.

3\. Communicate Clearly:

> o Instructors should clearly explain how grades will be distributed using the normal curve. This transparency helps manage student expectations and fosters understanding of how grading will work.

4\. Combine with Other Grading Methods:

> o The normal curve can be combined with other grading methods, such as rubrics for assignments, to ensure that students are evaluated comprehensively.

**19.6. Rationale for Using the Normal Curve in Grading**

• **Fairness and Objectivity:** The normal curve helps ensure that grades are distributed based on objective statistical measures, reflecting the performance relative to the class as a whole.

• **Prevents Grade Inflation:** By using statistical methods, it avoids artificially inflating grades in easy exams or assignments, maintaining academic rigor.

• **Encourages High Performance:** It encourages students to compete for the top grades, fostering a high-performance culture while also rewarding the highest achievers.

The Normal Curve in Grading provides a structured and fair method of distributing grades, especially in larger classes, by accounting for variations in performance and ensuring that grades reflect a relative standard. It supports the principles of fair competition and academic rigor while balancing the challenges of difficult exams and varying student abilities.

**20. Assessment in Higher Educational Institutes**

Assessment in higher education plays a critical role in evaluating students’ academic progress, understanding, and skill development. It provides both instructors and students with valuable feedback on their performance, guiding the learning process. This policy outlines the principles and practices for assessment in higher educational institutes, ensuring that assessments are fair, transparent, and aligned with educational objectives.

**20.1. Purpose of Assessment**

• **Evaluate Understanding:** The primary purpose of assessment is to measure students' understanding of the course material and their ability to apply knowledge in real-world contexts.

• **Feedback for Improvement:** Assessments serve as a tool for providing feedback to students about their strengths and areas for improvement. This allows students to enhance their learning and adjust their study strategies.

• **Guide Learning:** Continuous assessment encourages students to engage with the material consistently throughout the course, fostering a culture of active learning.

• **Certification of Learning:** Assessments help determine whether students have met the learning outcomes and are therefore eligible to progress in their studies or graduate.

**20.2. Types of Assessment**

1\. Formative Assessment:

• **Purpose:** Formative assessments are ongoing and occur during the learning process. Their primary purpose is to provide feedback to students so they can improve and adjust their learning strategies.

• **Examples:**

> o Quizzes and Assignments (online or written).
>
> o Discussions and Presentations.
>
> o Group Work and Peer Reviews.
>
> o Classroom Participation and Informal Polls.
>
> o Project Work and Research Activities.

2\. Summative Assessment:

• **Purpose:** Summative assessments are typically final evaluations of a student’s overall understanding and performance at the end of a module or course. They determine whether the student has achieved the course objectives.

• **Examples:**

> o Final Exams (Written or Oral).
>
> o End-of-Term Projects or Dissertations.
>
> o Mid-Term Exams and Final Quizzes.
>
> o Portfolios and Theses.

3\. Diagnostic Assessment:

• **Purpose:** Diagnostic assessments help identify students' prior knowledge and learning gaps before starting a course or topic. This allows instructors to adjust teaching methods and content accordingly.

• **Examples:**

> o Pre-tests or Diagnostic Surveys to gauge prior knowledge.
>
> o Self-assessments and Peer Assessments.

4\. Peer and Self-Assessment:

• **Purpose:** Peer and self-assessment promote self-reflection and collaborative learning. Students evaluate their own or others' work, which enhances their ability to critically assess academic work.

• **Examples:**

> o Peer reviews of written assignments.
>
> o Self-assessment rubrics for projects and presentations.

**20.3. Assessment Methods**

• **Written Examinations:**

Written exams, whether in the form of multiple-choice, short answer, or essay questions, test the student’s ability to recall and analyze information in a structured manner.

• **Oral Examinations:**

Oral exams assess the student's ability to explain and defend concepts verbally. They allow instructors to evaluate a student's critical thinking and communication skills.

• **Project-Based Assessment:**

Projects encourage students to apply their knowledge to solve practical, real-world problems. They allow for deeper learning and creativity.

• **Case Studies:**

In case-based assessments, students analyze and provide solutions to complex, real-world problems, which helps develop problem-solving and decision-making skills.

• **Presentations:**

Oral presentations allow students to convey their understanding clearly and succinctly, assessing both their knowledge and communication skills.

• **Laboratory Reports:**

In practical and lab-based courses, students are assessed on their ability to conduct experiments, analyze data, and report findings in a scientific manner.

**20.4. Grading and Marking**

• **Grading Systems:**

Different educational institutions use different grading systems, such as letter grades (A, B, C), percentage scores, or Grade Point Average (GPA). The grading system should be clear and consistently applied across all assessments.

• **Rubrics:**

Clear rubrics should be provided for all assignments, exams, and projects. Rubrics outline the criteria for each level of performance, ensuring that grading is objective, fair, and transparent.

• **Weightage of Assessment Components:**

The weightage of each assessment component should be clearly defined at the beginning of the course. For example:

> o **Mid-term exam:** 30%
>
> o **Final exam:** 40%
>
> o **Assignments and quizzes:** 30%

• **Re-grading and Rechecks:**

Students should have the option to request a recheck of their assessments, but this must be done through a formal process, which ensures fairness and accountability.

**20.5. Feedback and Evaluation**

• **Timely Feedback:**

Feedback should be provided promptly after the assessment. This enables students to reflect on their performance and learn from their mistakes.

• **Constructive Feedback:**

Feedback should be constructive and provide clear guidance on how students can improve. It should focus on strengths and areas for improvement.

• **Course Evaluation:**

At the end of the course, students should be encouraged to provide feedback on the teaching effectiveness, course content, and assessment methods. This feedback is crucial for course improvement.

**20.6. Ethical Considerations in Assessment**

• **Academic Integrity:**

All assessments should uphold the principles of academic integrity. Students should be evaluated based on their own work, and plagiarism, cheating, or any form of dishonest behavior should be dealt with severely.

• **Confidentiality:**

The results of assessments should be handled confidentially. Only authorized individuals (such as faculty members and academic staff) should have access to students' grades and personal academic data.

**20.7. Rationale for Assessment Policy**

• **Continuous Monitoring of Learning:** The policy promotes ongoing evaluation, allowing instructors to track student progress throughout the course and provide timely support.

• **Encouragement of Critical Thinking:** By incorporating diverse assessment methods (such as projects, case studies, and presentations), the policy encourages active learning, critical thinking, and real-world problem solving.

• **Transparency and Fairness:** Clear rubrics, consistent grading systems, and transparent feedback ensure that assessments are fair and that students understand how their performance is evaluated.

• **Preparation for Real-World Application:** The variety of assessment methods allows students to demonstrate their knowledge in practical, real-world contexts, preparing them for future careers.

This Assessment in Higher Educational Institutes policy ensures that students are evaluated fairly, continuously, and constructively, with opportunities for feedback and improvement. The policy supports the development of critical thinking, communication skills, and practical application of knowledge, contributing to overall student success and learning outcomes.

**21. Formative Assessment**

Formative assessment is a critical tool in the teaching and learning process, designed to monitor students' learning progress and provide ongoing feedback to improve their understanding and skills. Unlike summative assessments, which are conducted at the end of a course or unit to evaluate final performance, formative assessments occur throughout the learning process and are used to guide instructional strategies.

**21.1. Purpose of Formative Assessment**

• **Monitor Progress:** Formative assessments help instructors track student progress during the course and identify areas where students may be struggling.

• **Provide Feedback for Improvement:** The primary goal of formative assessment is to provide students with constructive feedback that can help them improve their understanding and performance before final evaluations.

• **Guide Instructional Adjustments:** The results of formative assessments allow instructors to adjust their teaching methods, course content, and pacing to better meet students' needs.

**21.2. Key Characteristics of Formative Assessment**

• **Ongoing and Regular:** Formative assessments are conducted continuously throughout the course, often during class activities, assignments, or discussions.

• **Non-Punitive:** These assessments are not graded in the traditional sense, but rather are meant to inform both the student and instructor about the learning process. The goal is improvement rather than judgment.

• **Focused on Learning:** The primary focus is on how students learn, providing an opportunity for them to engage with content and self-assess their learning.

**21.3. Methods of Formative Assessment**

1\. Quizzes and Mini-Assessments:

> o Short, frequent quizzes or online tests can be used to check students' understanding of key concepts after each lesson or module.
>
> o Quizzes can be multiple-choice, short answer, or true/false and should provide quick feedback on common misconceptions or knowledge gaps.

2\. Peer and Self-Assessment:

> o Peer reviews allow students to evaluate the work of their classmates, which enhances their understanding of the subject matter and promotes collaborative learning.
>
> o Self-assessment activities encourage students to reflect on their own work, evaluate their performance, and identify areas where they need improvement.

3\. Discussions and Group Work:

> o Classroom discussions or group work provide opportunities for students to actively engage with the material, share ideas, and learn from their peers.
>
> o Instructor-led discussions can be used to gauge student understanding in real time and guide them toward correct answers or deeper insights.

4\. In-Class Activities:

> o Interactive activities such as case studies, role-playing, and problem-solving exercises allow students to apply theoretical knowledge to practical scenarios.
>
> o These activities provide immediate insights into students' ability to think critically and analyse situations.

5\. Homework and Assignments:

> o Regular homework assignments or project work can be used to assess students’ understanding of the course content and their ability to apply concepts to real-world situations.
>
> o These assignments are not just a test of knowledge, but also a way to foster independent learning and critical thinking.

6\. Exit Tickets:

> o At the end of a class session, instructors can use exit tickets (short written responses) to ask students about the day's lesson, their understanding of the material, and any questions they may have.
>
> o This allows for immediate feedback and can guide the focus of the next lesson.

7\. Observations:

> o Instructors can use observations during class to assess students’ participation, engagement, and ability to apply concepts.
>
> o This method is particularly effective for assessing practical skills in lab courses or group settings.

**21.4. Feedback in Formative Assessment**

• **Timely Feedback:** Formative assessments must be followed by timely feedback, allowing students to understand what they did well and what areas need improvement.

• **Constructive and Specific:** Feedback should be specific, focusing on strengths and areas for improvement. It should encourage students to engage with the content and make adjustments in their learning approach.

• **Actionable:** Feedback should provide clear, actionable steps for students to improve. For example, instructors might suggest studying specific sections of the textbook, reviewing certain exercises, or engaging with additional resources.

• **Encouraging Reflection:** Students should be encouraged to reflect on the feedback and apply it to future learning tasks, fostering a growth mindset.

**21.5. Advantages of Formative Assessment**

• **Promotes Continuous Learning:** Formative assessments encourage students to stay actively engaged with the material throughout the course, rather than just at the end of the term.

• **Early Identification of Issues:** It allows both students and instructors to identify gaps in understanding early on, so there is enough time to address these issues before the final evaluation.

• **Improved Learning Outcomes:** When used effectively, formative assessments lead to better academic performance as students receive constant guidance and feedback, improving their ability to apply knowledge and skills.

• **Increases Student Engagement:** Students who receive continuous feedback are more likely to stay motivated and engaged, as they see their progress and understand how they can improve.

**21.6. Challenges of Formative Assessment**

• **Time and Effort:** Providing regular feedback through formative assessments requires significant time and effort from instructors. However, the benefits in terms of student learning and engagement outweigh this investment.

• **Student Resistance:** Some students may feel frustrated or demotivated by frequent assessments if they do not see the value in them. It is important for instructors to explain the purpose of formative assessments and how they contribute to overall learning.

• **Consistency in Feedback:** Instructors must ensure that feedback is consistent and fair across all students, providing equal opportunities for all to improve.

**21.7. Rationale for Formative Assessment Policy**

• **Encourages Growth:** Formative assessment focuses on student growth, not just final results. It allows for continuous improvement and helps students take ownership of their learning.

• **Enhanced Engagement:** By regularly assessing students, instructors can identify areas of interest and difficulty, tailoring their teaching methods to better meet student needs.

• **Alignment with Learning Outcomes:** Formative assessments align directly with course learning objectives, ensuring that students are developing the skills and knowledge necessary for success.

• **Promotes Active Learning:** Continuous engagement through formative assessments encourages students to be more active participants in their learning, rather than passive recipients of information.

This Formative Assessment Policy ensures that assessment is an integral part of the learning process, providing valuable insights for both students and instructors to improve teaching and learning outcomes. It focuses on continuous feedback, active participation, and student development throughout the course.

**22. Summative Assessment**

Summative assessment is a crucial component of the academic evaluation process, typically conducted at the end of an academic term, module, or course. Unlike formative assessments that focus on continuous learning and feedback, summative assessments evaluate the overall understanding, skills, and knowledge a student has gained over the course of the study period. This policy outlines the guidelines, types, and benefits of summative assessments in higher education.

**22.1. Purpose of Summative Assessment**

• **Measure Learning Outcomes:** The primary purpose of summative assessments is to measure the extent to which students have achieved the learning outcomes specified at the beginning of the course.

• **Final Evaluation:** Summative assessments provide a final evaluation of a student's performance in the course, offering a comprehensive overview of their academic progress.

• **Certification of Learning:** The results of summative assessments determine whether students are eligible to pass the course, progress to the next level, or graduate.

• **Comparison and Grading:** Summative assessments are typically used to assign grades that can be compared across a class, department, or institution, providing a metric for academic success.

**22.2. Types of Summative Assessment**

1\. Examinations:

> o **Written Exams:** Standard final exams that test students' knowledge through multiple-choice questions, short answers, and essay questions.
>
> o **Oral Exams:** Conducted to test students' verbal articulation, critical thinking, and ability to defend their ideas. This type is more common in oral communication, languages, or higher-level subject discussions.
>
> o **Take-Home Exams:** Exams that allow students to complete questions outside of class within a set time frame. These tests assess students' ability to conduct independent research and apply knowledge.
>
> o **Online Assessments:** With the rise of e-learning, many summative assessments are conducted through online platforms, allowing for secure testing of students’ knowledge in a controlled environment.

2\. Projects:

> o **Research Projects:** Research-based assignments that assess students’ ability to explore a topic deeply, conduct research, analyze data, and present findings. These often require a written report or presentation.
>
> o **Case Studies:** Students are given a case scenario and asked to apply theoretical knowledge to analyze, discuss, and propose solutions, testing their critical thinking and problem-solving abilities.

3\. Portfolios:

> o **Project Portfolios:** A collection of students' work over the semester, including drafts, assignments, reports, and reflections, compiled to demonstrate their overall learning process and growth.
>
> o **Creative Portfolios:** Typically used in courses involving art, design, or media, portfolios assess students’ final work and showcase their creative and technical skills.

4\. Dissertations and Theses:

> o **Dissertation/Thesis Projects:** A significant, independent research project that students must complete to demonstrate their ability to conduct academic research, present data, and contribute to their field of study.

5\. Presentations:

> o **Final Presentations:** A formal, oral presentation where students summarize their research findings, project outcomes, or overall understanding of the course material. This is often followed by a question-and-answer session with instructors or peers.

**22.3. Key Characteristics of Summative Assessment**

1\. Final Evaluation:

> o Summative assessments typically serve as a final measure of students' performance and are usually conducted at the end of a course or module.

2\. High Stakes:

> o The outcome of summative assessments often contributes significantly to students' final grades, making them high-stakes evaluations that have important academic consequences.

3\. Time-Bound:

> o Summative assessments are generally time-bound, meaning that students are required to complete the assessment within a fixed period, such as during an exam session or submission deadline for projects.

4\. Comprehensive:

> o These assessments often cover a broad range of topics from the course or module and require students to demonstrate a holistic understanding of the content.

5\. Objective and Transparent:

> o The grading criteria for summative assessments should be objective and clearly communicated to students ahead of time. This ensures that students understand how their performance will be evaluated.

**22.4. Designing Summative Assessment**

1\. Alignment with Learning Outcomes:

> o Summative assessments should be designed to align directly with the learning outcomes specified at the start of the course or module. The questions or tasks should test the knowledge, skills, and abilities that students are expected to have acquired.

2\. Variety of Question Types:

> o To ensure a fair and comprehensive assessment, summative assessments should use a variety of question types, such as:

• Multiple-choice questions to assess factual recall and understanding.

• Short-answer questions to test application of knowledge.

• Essay questions for critical thinking and analysis.

• Practical tasks or projects to evaluate hands-on skills and application.

3\. Grading Rubrics:

> o Clear grading rubrics should be established and shared with students before the assessment. These rubrics outline the criteria for each section of the assessment, specifying how marks will be awarded and ensuring transparency in grading.

4\. Time Allocation:

> o Ensure that the time allocated for completing the summative assessment is appropriate for the complexity of the tasks. In exams, the number of questions and the time available should be balanced to allow students to complete the work without unnecessary pressure.

5\. Feedback Mechanism:

> o After the summative assessment, feedback should be provided to students to help them understand their performance. This feedback can include comments on their strengths, areas of improvement, and general tips for future learning.

**22.5. Advantages of Summative Assessment**

1\. Final Evaluation: Summative assessments provide an accurate reflection of students’ overall understanding and ability to apply knowledge, making them a reliable measure of academic achievement.

2\. Certification: Summative assessments are used to determine if students meet the required standards to progress to the next stage or to graduate from the program.

3\. Accountability: They ensure that students take their learning seriously throughout the course and are held accountable for their academic progress.

4\. Comparison Across Cohorts: Summative assessments provide an opportunity to compare student performance across cohorts, courses, and institutions, contributing to the overall academic benchmarking.

**22.6. Challenges of Summative Assessment**

1\. Limited Feedback for Improvement: Unlike formative assessments, summative assessments typically provide limited opportunities for students to make real-time improvements before the final grading.

2\. Stressful: Because of the weight they carry in determining a student's final grade, summative assessments, especially exams, can be stressful and may not always reflect the true understanding of a student.

3\. Inflexibility: Summative assessments are generally one-time evaluations and may not be able to accommodate all learning styles, leading to unequal opportunities for all students.

**22.7. Rationale for Summative Assessment Policy**

• **Fair and Transparent Evaluation:** Summative assessments ensure that students are evaluated based on clear, predefined criteria, promoting fairness and transparency.

• **Alignment with Course Goals:** They ensure that students meet the learning outcomes of the course, helping institutions maintain academic standards and consistency in grading.

• **Certification and Progression:** Summative assessments are integral to determining student progression, certification, and eligibility for further academic opportunities.

This Summative Assessment Policy ensures that assessments are conducted in a manner that is both fair and comprehensive, aligning with the course’s learning outcomes and providing students with a final evaluation of their academic performance.

**23. Diagnostic Assessment**

Diagnostic assessment is an essential part of the learning process, primarily conducted before starting a course or a new topic. Its purpose is to assess students' existing knowledge, skills, and competencies, allowing instructors to identify gaps in learning and tailor their teaching approach accordingly. Unlike formative or summative assessments, diagnostic assessments are used to understand students' starting point and are not intended to contribute directly to final grades.

**23.1. Purpose of Diagnostic Assessment**

• **Identify Prior Knowledge:** Diagnostic assessments help instructors understand what students already know about the subject matter, ensuring that the teaching approach aligns with students' existing knowledge base.

• **Detect Learning Gaps:** These assessments identify gaps in students' understanding or areas that need more focus, allowing instructors to adjust the course material or pace accordingly.

• **Guide Teaching Strategies:** The results of diagnostic assessments enable instructors to tailor their teaching methods to the needs of the students, ensuring that no student is left behind.

• **Facilitate Personalized Learning:** By identifying each student’s strengths and weaknesses, diagnostic assessments help in designing a more personalized learning experience, allowing for differentiated instruction.

**23.2. Characteristics of Diagnostic Assessment**

1\. Pre-Assessment:

> o Diagnostic assessments are conducted before the instructional phase of a course or unit begins. They are used to gauge students' prior knowledge and understanding of key concepts.

2\. Non-Punitive:

> o These assessments are non-graded and do not contribute to the students' final grades. Their purpose is to inform instruction rather than to evaluate final performance.

3\. Formative in Nature:

> o Though diagnostic assessments are administered before the course, they share similarities with formative assessments in that they provide feedback to both students and instructors to guide future learning.

4\. Diagnostic in Purpose:

> o The primary goal is to diagnose students' existing competencies and weaknesses in the subject area, which helps in designing interventions for improving student learning.

**23.3. Types of Diagnostic Assessment**

1\. Pre-Tests:

> o These are short tests administered at the start of the course or unit. They assess students' familiarity with the subject matter and identify specific areas where they may need additional support.

2\. Surveys and Questionnaires:

> o Surveys or questionnaires can be used to gather information about students' learning styles, background knowledge, and prior experience with the topic. This helps instructors understand the learning needs and preferences of their students.

3\. Concept Maps:

> o Students may be asked to create concept maps showing how they organize and connect information. This helps instructors visually assess students' understanding of key concepts and the relationships between them.

4\. Classroom Discussions:

> o Informal discussions at the beginning of the course or unit allow students to share what they know about the topic. Instructors can use this information to gauge students' familiarity and identify knowledge gaps.

5\. Self-Assessment:

> o Instructors may ask students to evaluate their own skills and knowledge through self-assessment tools, enabling them to reflect on areas they feel confident about and areas where they might need help.

6\. Diagnostic Essays or Assignments:

> o In some cases, a short essay or assignment may be given to assess students’ writing skills, critical thinking, and subject knowledge. This can give instructors insight into the areas requiring further attention.

**23.4. How Diagnostic Assessment is Used**

1\. Informs Curriculum Design:

> o The results of diagnostic assessments provide instructors with valuable data to adjust their teaching content and delivery method. For example, if a diagnostic test reveals that many students lack a foundational understanding of a topic, the instructor may decide to revisit basic concepts before moving forward.

2\. Identifies Diverse Learning Needs:

> o Diagnostic assessments help instructors recognize the diverse learning needs of students, enabling them to differentiate instruction. This ensures that each student receives the support they need to succeed.

3\. Tailors Teaching Pace:

> o By understanding students' prior knowledge and learning gaps, instructors can adjust the pace of instruction. For example, if students already have a strong understanding of certain concepts, the instructor can spend less time on those areas and focus more on the difficult topics.

4\. Guides Student Support:

> o Diagnostic assessments help identify students who may require additional support, such as tutoring or remedial lessons, to ensure that all students are on track for success.

5\. Early Intervention:

> o By identifying gaps in knowledge early on, instructors can intervene before the student falls too far behind. This allows for timely academic support, whether through extra practice, additional resources, or one-on-one tutoring.

**23.5. Benefits of Diagnostic Assessment**

1\. Informs Instructional Strategies:

> o The primary benefit of diagnostic assessments is that they allow instructors to adjust their teaching methods, pace, and focus based on students' initial levels of understanding.

2\. Helps Identify Knowledge Gaps:

> o Diagnostic assessments provide valuable information about where students’ knowledge gaps lie, enabling instructors to address these gaps early in the course.

3\. Promotes Student Reflection:

> o When students engage in self-assessment or review their diagnostic test results, they are encouraged to reflect on their own learning needs and set goals for improvement.

4\. Prevents Wasted Instruction Time:

> o By identifying areas of strength and weakness, diagnostic assessments prevent instructors from spending unnecessary time on topics that students already understand. Instead, time can be spent reinforcing challenging concepts.

5\. Fosters Student Success:

> o Students benefit from a more tailored learning experience. When the course is aligned with their existing knowledge, they are more likely to succeed and feel confident in their learning.

**23.6. Challenges of Diagnostic Assessment**

1\. Time and Effort:

> o Designing and administering diagnostic assessments requires time and effort from instructors. Additionally, analyzing the results and adjusting the course accordingly can be time-consuming.

2\. Student Anxiety:

> o Although diagnostic assessments are non-graded, some students may still experience anxiety or stress, particularly if they are unaware of the purpose of these assessments. Instructors should explain the intent of diagnostic assessments clearly.

3\. Limited Scope:

> o Diagnostic assessments typically assess students' prior knowledge and may not always capture the full range of learning needs or the depth of understanding required for the course.

**23.7. Rationale for Diagnostic Assessment Policy**

• **Personalized Learning:** Diagnostic assessments support personalized learning by identifying each student’s starting point and tailoring the course to meet their needs.

• **Preventive Measure:** Early identification of gaps in knowledge through diagnostic assessment allows for early intervention, preventing academic struggles later in the course.

• **Improves Student Engagement:** By recognizing and addressing learning gaps, students are more likely to stay engaged and motivated to learn throughout the course.

This Diagnostic Assessment Policy ensures that the instructor has the tools to assess students' starting points, providing valuable insights to optimize the learning process and improve overall academic outcomes.

**24. Peer and Self-Assessment Policy**

Peer and self-assessment are powerful tools that enhance students' engagement, critical thinking, and reflective learning. By incorporating these assessments into the educational process, students gain a deeper understanding of the subject matter and develop valuable skills such as self-reflection, collaboration, and constructive feedback.

**24.1. Purpose of Peer and Self-Assessment**

• **Self-Reflection:** Self-assessment allows students to reflect on their own learning, identify strengths and weaknesses, and take responsibility for their academic progress.

• **Peer Learning:** Peer-assessment encourages students to learn from each other by providing feedback, discussing different perspectives, and analysing each other's work.

• **Development of Critical Skills:** Both peer and self-assessment foster important skills, including critical thinking, communication, and collaboration.

• **Improvement and Growth:** These assessments provide students with an opportunity to improve their performance through constructive feedback and self-analysis, contributing to continuous academic growth.

**24.2. Types of Peers and Self-Assessment**

1\. Self-Assessment:

> o **Description:** Self-assessment is when students evaluate their own work and reflect on their learning process. It encourages students to assess their own strengths, areas for improvement, and overall progress.
>
> o **Common Activities:**

• Reflection journals where students summarize what they have learned and identify areas of difficulty.

• Rubrics for students to evaluate their own assignments or projects.

• Progress reports where students assess their overall progress in the course.

2\. Peer-Assessment:

> o **Description:** Peer-assessment involves students assessing the work of their peers. This allows students to engage critically with their classmates' work, providing constructive feedback and gaining insights into how others approach the same problem.
>
> o **Common Activities:**

• Peer review of assignments, essays, or projects.

• Group project evaluations where students assess the contributions of their peers.

• Collaborative tasks where students work together and assess each other's performance and contributions.

**24.3. Benefits of Peer and Self-Assessment**

1\. Enhances Critical Thinking:

> o Both peer and self-assessment require students to critically analyze their own and others' work, improving their ability to evaluate arguments, ideas, and approaches.

2\. Encourages Active Learning:

> o When students assess their own work or that of their peers, they are more likely to engage actively with the material, making learning a more interactive and reflective process.

3\. Promotes Responsibility and Accountability:

> o Self-assessment encourages students to take ownership of their learning, while peer assessment promotes responsibility in providing valuable feedback to others.

4\. Develops Communication Skills:

> o Through providing feedback to their peers and reflecting on their own work, students develop strong communication skills—both in giving and receiving feedback.

5\. Fosters Collaboration:

> o Peer-assessment fosters collaborative learning, where students work together to improve their understanding of the material and learn from each other's strengths.

6\. Improves Academic Performance:

> o By reflecting on their own performance and receiving feedback, students can make targeted improvements in their work, leading to enhanced academic outcomes.

**24.4. Implementation Guidelines for Peer and Self-Assessment**

1\. Clear Guidelines:

> o To ensure that peer and self-assessments are effective, instructors should provide clear guidelines and rubrics for both self and peer evaluation. These guidelines should outline the criteria for assessment and ensure that students understand how to evaluate themselves and their peers fairly and constructively.

2\. Anonymous Feedback:

> o For peer-assessment, anonymity can help avoid bias and ensure that students feel comfortable providing honest feedback. Tools such as online platforms or peer assessment software can facilitate anonymous feedback exchange.

3\. Structured Reflection:

> o Self-assessment should be structured. Students should be guided on how to reflect on their work by focusing on specific aspects such as the quality of their ideas, the clarity of their arguments, and their use of resources.

4\. Feedback on Feedback:

> o Students should receive feedback on the feedback they provide to their peers. This helps improve the quality of peer reviews and ensures that students are giving constructive, helpful evaluations.

5\. Use of Rubrics:

> o Rubrics should be provided for both peer and self-assessment to ensure consistency in evaluations and to help students focus on key areas of performance. Rubrics outline the expected standards for various aspects of the work being evaluated.

6\. Integration into Grading:

> o While peer and self-assessment should primarily be used for feedback, grades can also be incorporated based on the quality of the self-assessment or peer reviews. This could be a part of participation or effort grades.

7\. Ongoing Assessment:

> o Peer and self-assessment should be ongoing and not just limited to one point in the course. Regular assessment allows students to track their progress and make continuous improvements.

**24.5. Challenges of Peer and Self-Assessment**

1\. Bias and Subjectivity:

> o Peer assessments can be influenced by personal biases, such as familiarity or friendships. To mitigate this, instructors should ensure that assessment criteria are clear and that students are trained to provide objective feedback.

2\. Inconsistent Feedback:

> o Some students may provide inconsistent or unhelpful feedback. This can be addressed by ensuring students understand the importance of constructive feedback and offering guidance on how to give it.

3\. Inaccurate Self-Assessment:

> o Students may sometimes overestimate or underestimate their own performance. To address this, instructors should compare self-assessment with actual performance and provide feedback to students on their ability to evaluate themselves accurately.

4\. Time and Effort:

> o Peer and self-assessment require additional time and effort from students. To ensure that these assessments do not overwhelm students, instructors should structure them in a manageable way and integrate them effectively into the course workload.

**24.6. Best Practices for Peer and Self-Assessment**

1\. Provide Clear Expectations: Clearly define the goals of peer and self-assessment so that students understand its purpose and how it will be used to enhance their learning.

2\. Create a Safe Environment: Encourage a positive and supportive environment where feedback is viewed as a learning tool rather than criticism.

3\. Ensure Transparency in Grading: Make sure students know how their peer and self-assessments will be graded, and ensure that this assessment aligns with the course's grading criteria.

4\. Offer Support and Guidance: Provide students with the tools and resources to give effective feedback. This can include examples of well-constructed feedback, tips on providing constructive criticism, and detailed rubrics.

**24.7. Rationale for Peer and Self-Assessment Policy**

• **Enhances Active Learning:** By incorporating peer and self-assessments, students become active participants in the learning process, allowing for deeper engagement and reflection on the material.

• **Develops Critical Thinking:** Both peer and self-assessment encourage students to engage in critical thinking, not only in evaluating their own work but also in analyzing the work of others.

• **Improves Communication:** Providing and receiving feedback strengthens students’ communication skills, which are essential in academic and professional settings.

• **Promotes Personal Growth:** Self-assessment, in particular, encourages students to take ownership of their learning, helping them identify their strengths and areas for improvement.

This Peer and Self-Assessment Policy ensures that students develop important academic and interpersonal skills such as critical thinking, communication, and self-reflection, while also improving their overall performance and engagement in the course.

**37. NSS/Outreach activities/ Teach the Community/ Extension Activities**

NSS volunteers generally work in villages, slums and voluntary agencies to complete 120 hours of regular activities during an academic year. As per the fundamental principles of National Service Scheme, a volunteer is expected to remain in constant touch with the community. Hence, it is of vital importance that a particular village/slum is selected for implementation of NSS programmes. As the NSS volunteer is to live with the members of the community during the 7 days Special Camping Programme and learn from their experience during his/her tenure in NSS, the village/slum should be carefully selected for adoption by NSS unit.

**37.1. Adoption of Villages**

1\. Adoption of a village and area is a very meaningful programme in NSS. It is far better to concentrate attention on one village and take up the task for development perspective, than to fritter away energy in many locations involving too many activities which may not be completed at all or where the follow up action may not be possible. From this point of view, village adoption programme should ensure continuity of work vis-a-vis sustained action, evaluation and follow up work.

**37.2. Contacting Village/Area Leaders**

2\. As a first step in this programme, it is necessary to establish contact with more than one village which would help to select a village where 'Leadership' is well established. In other words, selecting a village with proper leadership is very important as the sustained follow up action and evaluation is ensured in such places. To start with, the NSS unit can take the help of the Block Authorities, District Panchayat Officer, District Tribal Welfare Officer, District Medical Officer, Extension Officer of Agriculture, Irrigation and Education Departments for the selection of the village. It is to be noted that the selected villages should be within a short distance from the college so that constant contact can easily be made.

**37.3. Survey of the Village/Area**

3\. Before drawing up the plan of action, it is absolutely necessary to conduct a comprehensive survey of few villages situated at a short distance from the college. The assistance from the teachers and students of agriculture, economics, commerce, geography, statistics, home science, social work, medicine, psychology and education etc. have to be sought for the purpose. Conducting socio-economic survey can be an interesting field activity which has direct bearing on the curriculum of economics, commerce, statistics, psychology, health education etc. The report of such a survey will provide up-to-date information about the problems and potentials of the village and help in programme planning for village development. The applied field work will help the students to increase their analytical ability and deepen their thinking. Further, this will help them to identify the problems which have been left unnoticed. The survey work can also be accomplished with the help of PRA exercises (Participatory Rural Appraisal)

**37.4. Identification of Problem(s)**

4\. It is on the basis of this need assessment that projects/programmes are to be formulated. The programme officers should use their discretion and should identify the projects which can be completed by seeking assistance from the communities/other agencies.

5\. The aim of adoption of village or area is to give new ideas of development to the villagers which would improve their living conditions. Once the trust of the communities is won, they start cooperating with the NSS volunteers and approach them for solution of their problems. One of the important services that can be rendered by NSS volunteers is disseminating information about the latest developments in agriculture, watershed management, wastelands development, non-conventional energy, low cost housing, sanitation, nutrition and personal hygiene, schemes for skill development, income generation, government schemes such as Swachch Bharat, Ayushman Bharat, Accessible India, Digital India, Beti Bachao and Beti Padhao, Environment and Energy Conservation and Education, legal aid, consumer protection and allied field.

6\. The Programme Officers (PO) should motivate the communities to involve themselves with NSS for the community development work undertaken by the NSS unit. Further he or she will have to seek the help of various government departments and agencies for technical advice and financial assistance. Therefore, he/she must establish good rapport with the government officials and development agencies. For this, it is better if the administration is taken into confidence by prior consultations.

**37.5. Completion of Projects**

**37.7. Adoption of Slums**

Most of the colleges and universities are usually located in the urban areas. Due to long distance between the college campuses and the villages, the visits to the adopted villages by the NSS volunteers may become expensive and time consuming. In view of this, it is desirable to adopt slum especially by colleges located in urban areas.

**37.8. Survey of the Slum**

1\. For adoption of a slum, there should be composite survey team consisting of students drawn from faculties like, Arts, Science, Engineering, Medicine, Home Science and Social Work etc. The selected areas should be compact and should be easily accessible for students. Areas with acute political conflicts may be avoided.

2\. The issues pertaining to the identification of problems, project planning, interaction and coordination with the various departmental agencies, execution and completion of projects shall be undertaken on the same basis/lines as discussed earlier in the part 'Adoption of Villages'. The progress of projects should also be reviewed frequently.

**37.9. Services in Slums**

3\. The slum, tenements, Jhuggis and Jhoupris can be adopted by the NSS units with the aim of slum improvement. Under this, activities like providing water, water logging, sanitation, electricity, drainage, health and welfare services, life and living conditions, can be undertaken.

**37.10. NSS volunteers for Slum Work**

4\. Taking into account the living conditions & status of slum dwellers of the slums, only highly motivated, adaptable, mature and skilled students should be selected for slum development.

5\. Following are the tasks which the students can undertake in slum areas:

\(a\) As Community Investigators: They can prepare brief community profile on various slums in the city or town covering different amenities, services, and living conditions etc.

\(b\) As Community Workers: They can identify local leaders and in cooperation with them discuss local problems on which cooperative action can be initiated.

\(c\) As Programme Aides: Students can help the local communities' in launching a number of programmes like setting up a free milk distribution centre, sanitation drive, recreation, adult lad primary education, health projects like immunization. first aid centre, child care, nutrition classes, and free legal aid centres etc. They can also help in forming youth clubs, children's groups, mahila mandals etc.

\(d\) As Community Organizers: NSS students, after establishing rapport with the slum dwellers, can form community association to tackle local problems on a group basis with reliance on local resources, self-help and mutual aid and with some minimum external assistance.

**37.11. Some suggestions for Selection of slums**

\(i\) There should be a socio-economic survey of the slum by a team of volunteers drawn from different faculties;

\(ii\) The selected area should be compact. There should not be more than 300 residents in an adopted slum;

\(iii\) The community people should be receptive to the ideas of improving their living standard. They should also be ready to coordinate and involve in the projects undertaken by the NSS for their upliftment;

\(iv\) The areas where political conflicts are likely to arise should be avoided by the NSS units;

\(v\) The area should be easily accessible to the NSS volunteers to undertake frequent visits to slums;

\(vi\) The working in the slum needs commitment and hard work. Only sensitive and highly motivated NSS volunteers can find easy to serve in slum areas.

**38. Induction Programme**

This is a mandatory 2 week programme to be conducted as soon as the students enter the institution. Normal classes start only after the induction program is over.

The induction programme has been introduced by AICTE with the following objective: “Engineering colleges were established to train graduates well in the branch/department of admission, have a holistic outlook, and have a desire to work for national needs and beyond. The graduating student must have knowledge and skills in the area of his/her study. However, he/she must also have a broad understanding of society and relationships. Character needs to be nurtured as an essential quality by which he/she would understand and fulfil his/her responsibility as an engineer, a citizen and a human being. Besides the above, several metaskills and underlying values are needed.”

“One will have to work closely with the newly joined students in making them feel comfortable, allow them to explore their academic interests and activities, reduce competition and make them work for excellence, promote bonding within them, build relations between teachers and students, give a broader view of life, and build character”. Hence, the purpose of this programme is to make the students feel comfortable in their new environment, open them up, set a healthy daily routine, create bonding in the batch as well as between faculty and students, develop awareness, sensitivity and understanding of the self, people around them, society at large, and nature.

The following are the activities under the induction program in which the student would be fully engaged throughout the day for the entire duration of the program.

**(i) Physical Activity**

This would involve a daily routine of physical activity with games and sports, yoga, gardening, etc.

**(ii) Creative Arts**

Every student would choose one skill related to the arts whether visual arts or performing arts. Examples are painting, sculpture, pottery, music, dance etc. The student would pursue it everyday for the duration of the program. These would allow for creative expression. It would develop a sense of aesthetics and also enhance creativity which would, hopefully, grow into engineering design later.

**(iii) Universal Human Values**

This is the anchoring activity of the Induction Programme. It gets the student to explore oneself and allows one to experience the joy of learning, stand up to peer pressure, make decisions with courage, be aware of relationships with colleagues and supporting stay in the hostel and department, be sensitive to others, etc. A module in Universal Human Values provides the base. Methodology of teaching this content is extremely important. It must not be through do's and don'ts, but get students to explore and think by engaging them in a dialogue. It is best taught through group discussions and real life activities rather than lecturing. Discussions would be conducted in small groups of about 20 students with a faculty mentor each. It would be effective that the faculty mentor assigned is also the faculty advisor for the student for the full duration of the UG programme.

**(iv) Literary Activity**

Literary activity would encompass reading, writing and possibly, debating, enacting a play etc.

**(v) Proficiency Modules**

This would address some lacunas that students might have, for example, English, computer familiarity etc.

**(vi) Lectures by Eminent People**

Motivational lectures by eminent people from all walks of life should be arranged to give the students exposure to people who are socially active or in public life.

**(vii) Visits to Local Area**

A couple of visits to the landmarks of the city, or a hospital or orphanage could be organized. This would familiarize them with the area as well as expose them to the underprivileged.

**(viii) Familiarization to Dept./Branch & Innovations**

They should be told about what getting into a branch or department means what role it plays in society, through its technology. They should also be shown the laboratories, workshops & other facilities.

**(ix) Department Specific Activities**

About a week can be spent in introducing activities (games, quizzes, social interactions, small experiments, design thinking etc.) that are relevant to the particular branch of Engineering / Technology / Architecture that can serve as a motivation and kindle interest in building things (become a maker) in that particular field. This can be conducted in the form of a workshop.

For example, CSE and IT students may be introduced to activities that kindle computational thinking, and get them to build simple games. ECE students may be introduced to building simple circuits as an extension of their knowledge in Science, and so on. Students may be asked to build stuff using their knowledge of science. Induction Programme is totally an activity based programme and therefore there shall be no tests / assessments during this programme.

**41.1. Objectives**

The objective of introducing B.Tech. The programme is a best choice for academically excellent students having good academic record and interest towards higher studies and research.

**41.2. Key Characteristics**

• Higher academic standard than a general degree.

• Often includes a research project, dissertation, or thesis in the final year.

• May involve additional coursework or credit requirements.

• Typically awarded as a Bachelor of Arts (Hons) or Bachelor of Science (Hons).

**41.3. Academic Regulations for B.Tech. Honours Degree**

**Eligibility and Registration**

• Students can opt for an Honours program only in their own discipline/department.

• Must have a minimum SGPA of 8.0 up to 2nd semester with no backlogs to register at the beginning of the 4th semester.

• If 3rd semester SGPA drops below 8.0 post-registration, the Honours registration is automatically cancelled.

**Structure and Credit Requirements**

• Must complete all requirements of the regular B.Tech program plus 20 additional credits.

• 160 credits for major degree + 20 additional credits = 180 total.

• **Out of 20:**

> o 16 credits from four advanced domain-specific courses (each 4 credits).
>
> o 4 credits from two MOOCs (2 credits each, 8–12 weeks minimum duration).

**Course Guidelines**

• Courses selected must be from the student’s own discipline.

• Prerequisites must be completed before enrolling in a course.

• Courses may include theory and lab components (lab must be cleared separately).

• Virtual labs may be used for lab-based courses.

• Professional Electives can be chosen if not already studied in the B.Tech program.

**MOOCs**

• Must be minimum 8 weeks long.

• No attendance monitoring.

• Certificates from approved agencies required with grades or pass/fail.

• Pass/fail MOOCs will have grades as per university/academic council policy.

**Program Management**

• Minimum enrolment criteria set by BoS for course offering.

• If not met, equivalent MOOCs may be substituted with HoD & BoS approval.

• Time-tables for Honours must not clash with regular B.Tech schedule.

**Dropout/Failure Policy**

• Dropping or termination from Honours will render additional credits non-transferable.

• Courses will appear in the transcript (optionally with actual grades or "P") but not in degree certificate.

• CGPA below the requirement at any point will lead to removal from Honours program.

• Student will still receive a regular B.Tech with a separate grade sheet of extra courses.

**Restrictions and Final Award**

• No credit transfer between B.Tech and Honours programs.

• Must complete Honours simultaneously with the B.Tech degree.

• Cannot earn Honours after graduating with a regular degree.

• Student may withdraw from the Honours program and receive only the standard B.Tech degree.

**Examination and Evaluation**

• Follows same evaluation standards (internal, external, grading) as regular B.Tech.

• Withdrawal from semester end exams does not count as an attempt.

**Implementation**

• These regulations are applicable to students admitted from A.Y. 2025–2026 onwards.

> o Modification of existing laboratories
>
> o Revision of syllabus and curriculum
>
> o Addition/upgradation of infrastructural facilities
>
> o Execution is carried out by HoD and BoS for syllabi and course implementation.

• c. Role of Pro-Chancellor (Admin.)

> o Oversees infrastructure planning as per academic plan
>
> o Directs purchasing and construction-related activities
>
> o Responsible for monitoring and controlling infrastructure implementation.

• d. Phase 2 – Human Resources and Staffing

> o HoD and Dean plan faculty and staff requirements
>
> o Manage recruitment and staffing for academic programs
>
> o Monitor HR deployment across departments and activities.

• e. Phase 3 – Academic Operations and Scheduling

> o Led by HoD and Dean (Academics), covering:
>
> o Course and lab work allocation to faculty
>
> o Formation of divisional/departmental committees
>
> o Event identification (academic, research, outreach, etc.) with calendar dates
>
> o Coordination with other departments for inter-disciplinary teaching
>
> o Development and implementation of class and lab timetables.

• f. Monitoring and Audit

> o HoD conducts ongoing monitoring and academic audits
>
> o Divisional Academic Committee (DAC) performs overall academic auditing.

• g. Reporting and Corrective Actions

> o **HoD submits reports:**
>
> o Monthly progress report
>
> o Semester-wise performance review
>
> o Annual consolidated report
>
> o Reports include progress updates and corrective actions taken for deviations from plan.

**47. Review and Audit**

• a. Calendar and Audit Oversight

> o **A designated Professor In-charge will:**
>
> o Prepare a comprehensive calendar of events related to academic policy implementation.
>
> o Conduct audits of both major and minor academic activities/events.
>
> o Submit a monthly report to the Vice Chancellor (VC) and Chancellor.

• b. Standardization of Procedures

> o **The Professor In-charge is responsible for:**
>
> o Collecting current report formats, procedures, and processes from all divisions.
>
> o **Finalizing uniform templates and procedures for:**
>
> o Reporting formats
>
> o Academic processes
>
> o Review methodologies
>
> o Ensuring VC approval prior to issuing for department-wide compliance.

• c. Reporting of Findings and Recommendations

> o Review reports submitted by the Professor In-charge may include:
>
> o List of non-conformities to policy or procedures.
>
> o Suggested corrective actions for deviations.
>
> o Recommendations to improve institutional processes, events, and academic activities linked to the university’s policy.
