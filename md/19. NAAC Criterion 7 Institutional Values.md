**1. Introduction**

Institutional Values and Best Practices serve as the cornerstone of quality in higher education, shaping not only the academic and administrative landscape but also influencing the cultural and ethical fabric of an institution. They represent a holistic framework of guiding principles, ethical standards, sustainable approaches, and innovative practices that enable Higher Education Institutions (HEIs) to deliver on their mission of fostering knowledge, promoting inclusivity, ensuring sustainability, and contributing to societal development. By embedding values into everyday operations, HEIs establish a system of accountability, transparency, and continuous improvement, ensuring that their role extends beyond classrooms into the community, industry, and global networks. In the context of accreditation and quality assurance, such as those assessed under NAAC Criterion 7, institutional values and best practices form the foundation upon which an institution's credibility and long-term impact are evaluated.

At the core of institutional values lies integrity and ethics, which are essential for building trust among stakeholders. Integrity ensures that all decisions, whether academic or administrative, are taken with honesty and transparency. It requires institutions to uphold accountability in financial management, admissions, recruitment, and academic evaluation. Ethical practices also extend to research, ensuring that plagiarism is prevented, intellectual property rights are respected, and data collection is done responsibly. A transparent governance model that fosters participatory decision-making among faculty, staff, students, and management embodies the principle of integrity. Institutions that maintain high ethical standards are able to create a culture of credibility, ensuring that their stakeholders can rely on the fairness and honesty of their operations.

Closely aligned with ethics is the principle of inclusivity and diversity, which recognizes the importance of creating an equitable and welcoming environment for all. In a multicultural society like India, where disparities based on caste, gender, language, and socio-economic backgrounds are prevalent, inclusivity becomes a non-negotiable value for HEIs. Institutions are expected to provide equal opportunities in admissions, scholarships, placements, and faculty recruitment. Beyond formal policies, inclusivity must also translate into everyday practices—ensuring safe spaces for women and marginalized groups, establishing grievance redressal mechanisms, promoting gender sensitization workshops, and offering bridge courses for students from disadvantaged backgrounds. Diversity enriches the learning experience by exposing students to multiple perspectives and helps in building global citizens capable of working in multicultural environments. Thus, inclusivity is not merely about compliance with regulations but about fostering a community where differences are respected and celebrated.

Another cornerstone of institutional values is sustainability and environmental responsibility, which has gained significant importance in the era of climate change and global environmental crises. Higher education institutions serve as both consumers of resources and influencers of societal behavior. Therefore, they are expected to adopt eco-friendly practices that reduce their ecological footprint while also sensitizing students and communities about environmental stewardship. Green and Clean Campus Initiatives are exemplary in this regard, involving activities like tree plantation drives, rainwater harvesting, waste segregation, composting of biodegradable waste, banning single-use plastics, promoting bicycle usage, and installing solar power systems to reduce dependency on non-renewable energy. By embedding sustainability in their physical infrastructure, curriculum, and research priorities, institutions prepare students to become environmentally responsible citizens. Furthermore, green practices directly connect HEIs to national and global goals such as India's Swachh Bharat Mission and the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs).

Excellence in education is another critical institutional value that underpins the mission of HEIs. Excellence goes beyond classroom instruction and encompasses quality in teaching, research, innovation, and student learning outcomes. Institutions are expected to continually upgrade their pedagogy by incorporating ICT-enabled learning, flipped classrooms, experiential learning, and outcome-based education. Faculty development programmes ensure that teaching staff remain abreast of the latest knowledge in their disciplines, while research grants and incubation centers support a culture of innovation and discovery. Excellence also entails creating opportunities for interdisciplinary learning, promoting global exposure through exchange programmes, and ensuring robust evaluation mechanisms that test not just rote learning but critical thinking and application skills. An institution committed to excellence ensures that its graduates are employable, entrepreneurial, and capable of lifelong learning.

Complementing academic excellence is the value of social responsibility, which positions HEIs as agents of community development and societal transformation. Institutions must go beyond their academic boundaries and address the needs of the communities they are part of. Social responsibility manifests in community service projects, extension activities, rural development initiatives, financial literacy camps, health and sanitation awareness drives, and partnerships with NGOs for social welfare programmes. Through platforms such as the National Service Scheme (NSS), Red Ribbon Clubs, and community service internships, students are given opportunities to contribute to society while also enhancing their empathy and leadership skills. These engagements help inculcate values of compassion, civic responsibility, and ethical citizenship among students, ensuring that they emerge not only as successful professionals but also as socially conscious human beings.

The value of collaboration and networking further enhances institutional best practices by connecting HEIs with industry, government, civil society, and international academia. Partnerships with industries provide students with internships, training, and employment opportunities, thereby bridging the gap between academia and the professional world. Collaborations with government bodies facilitate research projects, consultancy, and community development initiatives. International partnerships allow student and faculty exchanges, joint research, and exposure to global best practices. Networking strengthens the institution's resource base, enhances its reputation, and creates opportunities for cross-cultural learning and problem-solving. In today's interconnected world, no institution can thrive in isolation, and hence collaboration becomes an indispensable institutional value.

These values are operationalized through best practices, which are innovative, sustainable, and replicable methodologies that enhance institutional effectiveness. Transparent governance ensures participatory decision-making and effective leadership. Innovations in teaching and learning include blended learning models, digital platforms, and learner-centered pedagogies. A strong research culture is fostered through seed money for faculty research, incubation support for student startups, and partnerships for applied research. Comprehensive student support systems such as counseling centers, mentorship programmes, skill development initiatives, and financial aid schemes ensure the holistic development of learners. Active community engagement keeps institutions rooted in societal realities and strengthens their role as agents of change.

One of the most widely recognized best practices under NAAC's Criterion 7 is the implementation of Green and Clean Campus Initiatives. These initiatives are both visible and

measurable, making them ideal for showcasing an institution's commitment to sustainability. Planting trees and creating green zones contribute to biodiversity. Rainwater harvesting systems reduce dependency on external water sources. Solar power installations lower carbon emissions and energy costs. Waste segregation and composting promote a circular economy within the campus. Plastic-free policies reduce pollution and set behavioral examples for students and staff. These practices not only make campuses environmentally friendly but also serve as living laboratories where students learn about sustainability through real-life applications.

The effectiveness of institutional values and best practices depends on robust monitoring and evaluation mechanisms. Internal quality assurance cells (IQACs) are responsible for conducting periodic audits to assess the implementation of policies and practices. Feedback systems involving students, faculty, alumni, and community stakeholders ensure that practices remain relevant and effective. Participation in national and international ranking frameworks like NIRF, ARIIA, and QS I-GAUGE provides external validation of institutional performance and pushes institutions towards continuous improvement. Benchmarking against peer institutions helps identify gaps and areas for innovation. By embedding monitoring into their framework, HEIs ensure that best practices are not one-time efforts but part of a continuous cycle of planning, implementation, assessment, and refinement.

In conclusion, Institutional Values and Best Practices are the pillars upon which higher education institutions build their credibility, relevance, and impact. They guide institutions in maintaining integrity, promoting inclusivity, achieving academic excellence, fulfilling social responsibility, and embracing sustainability. Best practices transform these values into tangible actions that create measurable outcomes for students, faculty, communities, and the environment. Together, they ensure that institutions are not only centers of knowledge but also agents of positive change, aligned with both national priorities and global development goals. By embedding these principles into their fabric, HEIs contribute to building a more equitable, sustainable, and progressive society, fulfilling their responsibility as custodians of knowledge and catalysts of transformation.

**34. Differently-abled (Divyangjan) Friendly, Barrier-Free Environment**

The institution is committed to ensuring an inclusive, accessible, and equitable learning environment for all students, faculty, and visitors, including persons with disabilities (Divyangjan). Our campus design, facilities, and services are aligned with the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016, the UGC Accessibility Guidelines, and universal design principles to provide a truly barrier-free environment.

**1. Built Environment for Physical Accessibility**

> • **Ramps & Lifts:** All main academic blocks, administrative offices, library, laboratories, and hostels are equipped with gently sloped ramps and barrier-free entrances.
>
> • **Lifts:** Multi-storey buildings are fitted with lifts designed for wheelchair users, including braille buttons and audio floor announcements for visually impaired persons.
>
> • **Wide Doorways & Corridors:** To facilitate wheelchair movement, all new constructions follow the minimum door width and turning radius standards.

**2. Divyangjan-Friendly Washrooms**

> • Dedicated accessible washrooms are available in each academic and residential block.
>
> • Facilities include grab bars, anti-slip flooring, lever-type taps, and lowered sinks for wheelchair users.
>
> • Washrooms are clearly marked with accessibility signage in both text and braille.

**3. Signage & Navigation Support**

> • **Tactile Pathways:** Tactile tiles have been laid from main entry points to classrooms, the library, administrative blocks, and restrooms.
>
> • **Signage:** Large, high-contrast, and braille-enabled signboards are installed at key locations for easy navigation.
>
> • **Illumination:** Pathways and corridors are well-lit to assist individuals with low vision.
>
> • **Directional Signposts:** Installed at all junctions to guide Divyangjan users.

**4. Assistive Technology & Digital Accessibility**

> • **Accessible Website:** The institutional website is compliant with WCAG 2.1 accessibility standards, offering text-to-speech, adjustable font size, and high-contrast mode.
>
> • **Screen-Reading Software:** Public computer systems in the library are equipped with JAWS/NVDA screen-reading software.
>
> • **Mechanized Equipment:** Adjustable-height desks, motorized projectors, and assistive hearing devices are available for classroom use.
>
> • **Digital Learning Materials:** Soft copies of lecture notes, e-books, and recorded lectures are provided for ease of access.

**5. Provision for Enquiry & Learning Assistance**

> • **Help Desks:** Staffed information counters provide human assistance for navigation and access to facilities.
>
> • **Reader & Scribe Services:** Available during examinations and academic work, as per Divyangjan requirements.
>
> • **Study Material Support:** Soft copies in accessible formats, enlarged font printouts, and audio materials are made available on request.
>
> • **Counselling & Guidance:** A designated faculty coordinator assists Divyangjan students with academic, personal, and administrative needs.

**6. Policy & Monitoring**

> • The Institutional Accessibility Policy ensures ongoing improvements in Divyangjan infrastructure and support services.
>
> • Regular feedback from Divyangjan students is taken to address emerging needs.

**61. Best Practice 1: Digital Academic & Administrative Management System (DAAMS)**

**1. Title of the Practice**

Digital Academic & Administrative Management System (DAAMS)

**2. Objectives of the Practice**

> • To integrate academic and administrative functions under a single digital platform.
>
> • To enhance transparency, accuracy, and speed in institutional operations.
>
> • To provide real-time access to data for students, faculty, and administration.
>
> • To promote paperless governance in alignment with sustainability goals.
>
> • To strengthen decision-making through data analytics and reporting.

**3. The Context**

Previously, student records, faculty workload, and administrative approvals were maintained manually, leading to delays, redundancy, and errors. Academic planning, attendance, and examination management were handled through separate systems, causing disjointed operations. The institution required an integrated solution to centralize data, minimize duplication, and enable efficient monitoring. Moreover, compliance with NAAC, NIRF, and other accreditation requirements demanded a data repository that was easily retrievable. The pandemic further accelerated the need for a robust digital system to support remote and hybrid operations.

**4. The Practice**

The DAAMS platform was designed and implemented to integrate admissions, attendance, examinations, accounts, and library functions. The ERP includes:

> • Faculty Portal for academic planning, lesson uploads, and attendance entry.
>
> • Student Portal for timetable, grades, assignments, and fee payments.
>
> • Digital File Movement System for online approvals with digital signatures.
>
> • Library Management System (KOHA) for automated cataloguing and circulation.

The system enables administrators to monitor performance, track student progress, and generate statutory reports. Data security is ensured through encrypted cloud storage and regular backups.

> • **Unique Feature:** Real-time data dashboards for decision-making and compliance reporting.
>
> • **Constraints:** Initial investment in software licensing and ICT infrastructure; user training requirements.

**5. Evidence of Success**

> • 95% reduction in paper use, saving over ₹2 lakhs annually.
>
> • Approval turnaround time reduced by 50%.
>
> • Academic & Administrative Audit reports note improved efficiency.
>
> • Student satisfaction scores in the annual feedback improved from 78% to 92% in the “Ease of Access to Information” parameter.

**6. Problems Encountered and Resources Required**

> • Resistance from non-technical staff during the initial phase.
>
> • Need for continuous ICT training and handholding.
>
> • Resources required: ERP licensing, server infrastructure, IT support staff.

**7. Notes (Optional)**

The DAAMS model is scalable and can be replicated by other institutions. Integration with national academic repositories such as NAD and DigiLocker is planned.

**3. Metric 7.1.6: Quality Audits on Environment & Energy**

Statement: The institution should demonstrate that environment and energy-related quality audits are regularly undertaken and initiatives are in place for sustainability.

**Sub-Components to be Confirmed (7.1.6.1)**

1\. Green Audit / Environment Audit

> o Independent review of campus greenery, biodiversity, and sustainable practices.
>
> o Includes waste management, carbon footprint, air & water quality checks.

2\. Energy Audit

> o Analysis of electricity consumption, efficiency of lighting, HVAC, lab equipment.
>
> o Recommendations on energy-saving and renewable adoption.

3\. Clean and Green Campus Initiatives

> o Tree plantation, solid waste management, plastic-free initiatives, zero-litter drives.
>
> o Awareness campaigns with student involvement.

4\. Beyond the Campus Initiatives

> o Community awareness programs: water conservation, renewable energy awareness, tree plantations in nearby villages, clean drives in public areas.

**Documents Required for Verification**

> • Policy Document on Environment & Energy usage.
>
> • Certificates / Reports from Recognized Auditing Agencies (Green, Energy, Environment audits).
>
> • Awards & Recognition Certificates (if received from external bodies).
>
> • Reports of Activities (with dates, captions, and details).
>
> • Geo-tagged Photographs (with clear labels: event, date, location).
>
> • Institutional Website Links (direct URLs to evidence page, not homepage).

**Specific Instructions to HEIs**

> • All audits should be carried out by recognized agencies (Govt. bodies, ISO-certified firms, NGOs).
>
> • The same should be repeated year-wise for the entire five-year assessment period.
>
> • Activities only within campus are valid for Green & Energy Audits.
>
> • Activities beyond campus must show community involvement (not limited to internal students/staff).
>
> • Evidence must be digitally hosted on the institution website with specific URL, not generic homepage.
>
> • Certificates/Audits must be dated and fall within the assessment period.

**Not to be Considered**

> • Backup diesel generators (not green/renewable).
>
> • Events without proof (photos, reports, certificates).
>
> • Informal activities without proper documentation.
>
> • Generic website links (homepage only).

**4. Metric 7.1.10 – Code of Conduct**

**Objective:**

To ensure the institution prescribes, monitors, and promotes adherence to a Code of Conduct for students, teachers, administrators, and staff, while also conducting awareness/ethics programs.

**Key Components Required**

1\. Institutional Code of Conduct principles displayed on the website

> o Policy document/approved code of ethics.
>
> o Particular URL (not just homepage).
>
> o Evidence: Screenshot of webpage, web link.

2\. Committee to monitor adherence

> o Constitution of a Code of Conduct Monitoring Committee.
>
> o Proceedings/minutes of meetings.
>
> o Evidence: Copy of order/notification, committee reports.

3\. Professional ethics programmes

> o Organized for students, teachers, administrators, staff.
>
> o Evidence: Circulars, attendance lists, programme schedules, geo-tagged photos with captions and dates.

4\. Annual awareness programmes

> o Workshops/seminars/orientations on professional ethics and code of conduct.
>
> o Evidence: Report of activities with outcomes, participant feedback.

**Documents Required**

> • Policy document on Code of Ethics / Code of Conduct.
>
> • Constitution and proceedings of the monitoring committee.
>
> • Circulars, invitations, and reports of professional ethics programmes.
>
> • Handbooks, manuals, or brochures on human values & ethics.
>
> • Report on student attributes/graduate attributes facilitated by the Institution.
>
> • Geo-tagged photos with captions/dates of awareness programmes.
>
> • Website link where the Code of Conduct is displayed.

**Not to be Included**

> • Generic website homepage link (must be direct link).
>
> • Informal/unrecorded programmes without proper evidence.
>
> • Non-approved drafts of the Code of Conduct.
