## Abstract

Criterion 9, titled *“Student Support System and Governance,”* is one of the most pivotal components of the NBA accreditation framework, carrying a substantial weightage of 120 marks distributed across 14 sub-criteria. This criterion reflects the NBA’s commitment to ensuring that institutions operate with a strong student-centric philosophy, transparent and accountable governance, efficient resource management, and a culture of innovation and sustainability. It not only measures the adequacy of institutional structures in supporting students’ academic, professional, and personal development but also emphasizes the alignment of governance processes with long-term institutional excellence and global development priorities.

At the foundation of this criterion lies the assurance of effective student support mechanisms. The First-Year Student–Faculty Ratio (FYSFR) is assessed to ensure that adequate faculty resources are available to guide first-year undergraduate students, who often require the most academic and emotional support during their transition into higher education. Complementing this, a formalized Mentoring System is evaluated, which provides structured academic, professional, and personal guidance to students. This mentorship framework enables early identification of challenges, timely interventions, and holistic student development. A robust Feedback Analysis system forms another core element, where institutions are required to systematically collect, analyze, and act upon student feedback related to teaching–learning processes, academic facilities, and campus life. Training and Placement support is also assessed under this criterion, with emphasis on career guidance, industry collaborations, internship facilitation, and placement outcomes. In addition, Criterion 9 recognizes the growing importance of entrepreneurship, encouraging institutions to establish incubation centers, provide seed funding, and promote student start-ups as part of their entrepreneurial ecosystem.

Governance and transparency form a major dimension of this evaluation. Institutions are expected to demonstrate a well-defined strategic plan and present clear evidence of its systematic implementation. Governing body structures, administrative setups, service rules, and recruitment and promotion policies must be transparent, equitable, and in alignment with national regulations. Public disclosure of essential information, including policies, academic calendars, faculty details, and financial statements, is a mandatory requirement, reflecting the principles of accountability and openness. Financial management is scrutinized through evidence of year-wise budget allocation and utilization at both institutional and program levels, with strict requirements for public accounting and the maintenance of audited expenditure records. These practices ensure not only fiscal discipline but also trust-building among stakeholders.

The quality of learning resources represents another critical sub-criterion. Institutions must demonstrate the adequacy and accessibility of both physical resources, such as libraries, laboratories, and workshops, and digital resources, including e-journals, repositories like DSpace, and advanced Learning Management Systems (LMS). The increasing emphasis on E-Governance is also highlighted, where institutions are encouraged to adopt digital platforms for administration, academics, and communication to ensure efficiency, transparency, and accountability.

Sustainability and innovation are integrated into Criterion 9 through the evaluation of institutional initiatives aligned with the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). This includes evidence of green campus practices such as renewable energy adoption, waste reduction, and water conservation, as well as research and development activities linked to sustainability challenges. Outreach programs that engage students and faculty in community development projects also fall within this ambit, ensuring that the institution contributes directly to societal well-being. Similarly, innovative educational practices, such as the Academic Bank of Credits (ABC), multidisciplinary curricula, and flexible learning pathways, are assessed for their effectiveness in enhancing student learning opportunities in line with the National Education Policy (NEP) 2020.

The Faculty Performance Appraisal and Development System (FPADS) is another vital component under this criterion. Institutions are required to demonstrate structured mechanisms for assessing faculty performance, recognizing excellence, and providing opportunities for professional growth. This not only contributes to faculty development but also ensures that high-quality teaching, research, and mentoring are sustained over time. Additionally, outreach and extension activities form an essential sub-criterion, with institutions expected to actively engage students in community service, awareness campaigns, and societal impact initiatives, thereby instilling values of civic responsibility and social accountability.

A unifying theme across all 14 sub-criteria of Criterion 9 is the emphasis on structured processes, robust documentation, and demonstrable evidence of impact. Institutions are required to show measurable improvements in academic performance indicators such as student success rates, reduction in dropout rates, placement outcomes, entrepreneurial ventures, and student satisfaction levels. Moreover, tangible contributions to national priorities and global development agendas, including the SDGs, must be highlighted through documented evidence and outcome reports. By demanding accountability, transparency, and impact-driven processes, Criterion 9 positions itself as a cornerstone of NBA accreditation, ensuring that institutions function not only as centers of learning but also as engines of social transformation, innovation, and sustainable development.

# 1. Introduction

Criterion 9, titled “Student Support System and Governance,” is a critical component of the NBA accreditation framework, carrying a substantial weight of 120 marks out of the total. This criterion reflects the holistic approach of the NBA in ensuring that higher education institutions not only deliver academic excellence but also provide a comprehensive ecosystem that supports students’ academic, professional, and personal development. It evaluates the extent to which institutions foster a student-centric environment, establish transparent and accountable governance practices, manage financial resources efficiently, provide access to high-quality learning resources, encourage innovation and entrepreneurship, and engage meaningfully with the wider community. By doing so, Criterion 9 positions the institution as more than just a place of instruction–it becomes a hub of student empowerment, institutional accountability, and social responsibility.

The scope of Criterion 9 is broad and multi-dimensional, covering diverse yet interconnected areas of institutional functioning. Student support is assessed through parameters such as the First Year Student–Faculty Ratio (FYSFR), which ensures adequate faculty availability for mentoring and guiding first-year undergraduates, and a structured Mentoring System that provides academic, professional, and personal support. A systematic Feedback Analysis mechanism is also reviewed, focusing on how institutions collect, analyze, and act on student feedback to improve teaching–learning processes and academic facilities. Dedicated support for Training and Placement is an essential part of this criterion, alongside institutional initiatives to foster start-ups and entrepreneurship through incubation centers, seed funding, and entrepreneurial training programs.

Governance and transparency form another cornerstone of this criterion, accounting for 25 marks. Institutions must present evidence of a strategic plan and its systematic implementation, clarity in governing body structures, well-defined administrative setups, service rules, recruitment and promotion policies, and the public disclosure of all essential information. Financial management is equally important, with Criterion 9 requiring proof of budget allocation and utilization at both institute and program levels. Institutions are mandated to maintain year-wise audited expenditure records and ensure mandatory public accounting, reflecting fiscal responsibility and accountability to stakeholders.

The quality and accessibility of learning resources is also emphasized under Criterion 9. Institutions must demonstrate the availability of adequate physical resources such as libraries and laboratories, as well as digital resources including e-journals, repositories like DSpace, and Learning Management Systems (LMS). At the same time, the adoption of E-Governance practices is evaluated to ensure efficiency, transparency, and digital integration in academic and administrative operations. The criterion also highlights the importance of sustainability and innovation, encouraging institutions to align their academic and research activities with the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). Evidence of green campus practices, R&D projects on sustainability, outreach programs, and innovative educational initiatives such as the Academic Bank of Credits and multidisciplinary curricula are expected to be demonstrated.

Finally, faculty development and outreach activities form an integral part of Criterion 9. The Faculty Performance Appraisal and Development System (FPADS) is assessed for its effectiveness in enhancing faculty growth, supporting research, and contributing to institutional development. Outreach activities, such as student participation in NSS, NCC, and community service programs, are reviewed as evidence of the institution’s societal commitment. A consistent theme across all sub-criteria is the emphasis on structured processes, robust documentation, demonstrable evidence, and measurable outcomes. Institutions are expected to showcase tangible improvements in areas such as academic performance, reduction in dropout rates, placement outcomes, student satisfaction, and contributions to both national priorities and global development goals. In essence, Criterion 9 underscores the idea that a truly accredited institution is not only academically strong but also student-centered, transparent, financially accountable, innovative, and socially responsible.

# **2. Criterion 9:** Student Support System and Governance

This criterion measures how well the institution provides student-centric support systems, transparent governance, effective financial management, quality learning resources, innovation, and outreach.

### 9.1. First Year Student-Faculty Ratio (FYSFR) (05 marks)

- Ensures sufficient faculty for first-year UG students.

- **Formula:** FYSFR = Student intake ÷ Faculty count (weighted system NS1×0.8 + NS2×0.2 vs RF4).

- **Marks:** ≥90% compliance = 5; 80–89% = 4; 70–79% = 3; 60–69% = 2; 50–59% = 1; \<50% = 0.

- **Evidence:** Admission data, faculty list, timetables, workload distribution.

- Mentor–mentee allocation, professional and personal counselling.

- Includes career guidance, remedial support, senior–junior mentoring.

- **Marks based on:** coverage of students, frequency of meetings, and impact.

- **Evidence:** Mentor logbooks, counselling reports, impact analysis (dropout reduction, placement rise).

Structured system for collecting, analyzing, and acting on feedback.

# 3. First Year Student-Faculty Ratio (FYSFR)

## 1. Purpose of FYSFR in NBA

- The First Year Student-Faculty Ratio (FYSFR) is an important quantitative indicator in NBA accreditation.

- It measures whether adequate faculty members are available to handle the first-year intake of students, ensuring quality in teaching and mentoring at the foundational level.

- A lower ratio (i.e., fewer students per faculty) indicates better personalized attention and strong academic support in the crucial first year.

## 2. Formula for FYSFR (NBA Standard)

FYSFR=X/Y

**Where:**

- X = Total number of students admitted in first year across all programs (UG only).

- Y = Total number of faculty members exclusively handling first-year courses.

**Note:** Only regular/approved faculty members are counted (not visiting/part-time).

## 3. NBA Norms for FYSFR (Scoring Criteria)

- **NBA has prescribed norms and assigns marks accordingly:**

| **FYSFR (Students per Faculty)** | **Marks Awarded (out of 5)** |
|----------------------------------|------------------------------|
| ≤ 20                             | 5                            |
| \> 20 and ≤ 25                   | 4                            |
| \> 25 and ≤ 30                   | 3                            |
| \> 30 and ≤ 35                   | 2                            |
| \> 35 and ≤ 40                   | 1                            |
| \> 40                            | 0                            |

## 4. Example Calculation

- Suppose an institute admits 360 students in first year (X = 360).

- There are 18 dedicated first-year faculty members (Y = 18).

- **Then:**

> FYSFR=360/18=20

- Score = 5 marks (since FYSFR ≤ 20).

## 5. Evidence Required for NBA

- Admission Records (student strength in CFY).

- Faculty List with designation, qualifications, and workload (showing allocation to first-year courses).

- Time Tables (mapping faculty to first-year subjects).

- Approval Letters (AICTE/University-approved faculty strength).

## 6. NBA Expectation

- FYSFR must be within acceptable limits (preferably ≤ 25).

- **If ratio is high (e.g., \>30), institute should show corrective measures:**

<!-- -->

- Recruitment of additional faculty.

- Redistribution of workload.

<!-- -->

- Consistency in last 3 years (CAY, CAYm1, CAYm2) should be demonstrated.

# 4. First Year Student-Faculty Ratio (FYSFR) with detailed calculation process using the given framework

## 1. Parameters & Definitions

- S4 = Sanctioned intake of all UG programs (first-year students admitted).

- RF4 = Required faculty = S4 ÷ 20 (as per 1:20 faculty-student ratio).

- NS1 = No. of faculty in *Basic Sciences, Humanities, Social Sciences, Management*.

- NS2 = No. of faculty in *Engineering Sciences*.

- Weighted Faculty Count = (NS1 × 0.8) + (NS2 × 0.2).

- Percentage Coverage = (Weighted Faculty Count ÷ RF4) × 100.

## 2. Calculation Logic

Percentage=(NS1×0.8)+(NS2×0.2)RF4×100

- This formula accounts for the weightage of basic sciences vs engineering science faculty in teaching first-year students.

- A higher percentage means better compliance with faculty norms.

## 3. Example (from your data)

*CAY (Current Academic Year)*

- S4 = 300

- RF4 = 300 ÷ 20 = 15

- NS1 = 18, NS2 = 3

- Weighted Faculty = (18×0.8) + (3×0.2) = 14.4 + 0.6 = 15

# 5. Mentoring System

Scope (5 Marks Component)

- NBA evaluates whether the institution has a formal mentoring framework to support students academically, professionally, and personally.

- Marks are awarded based on existence, implementation, monitoring, and effectiveness of mentoring.

## 1. Types of Mentoring

> 1\. Professional Guidance

- Career counseling, higher studies guidance, competitive exams preparation (GATE, GRE, UPSC, etc.).

- Industry exposure and networking through alumni.

> 2\. Career Advancement

- Placement preparation, internships, skill development programs.

- Guidance on resume building, soft skills, interview training.

> 3\. Coursework-Specific Mentoring

- Helping students understand subject concepts, assignments, exam preparation.

- Support for remedial classes and slow learners.

> 4\. Laboratory-Specific Mentoring

- Hands-on guidance during lab sessions.

- Research/project mentoring for mini-projects and final year projects.

> 5\. All-Round Development

- Guidance on co-curricular and extracurricular activities.

- Support in managing stress, wellness, and life skills.

## 2. Structure of Mentoring System

- **Faculty Mentors Assigned:** Each faculty member mentors a fixed number of students.

- **Ratio:** Typically, 1 mentor per 15–20 students.

- **Frequency of Meetings:**

<!-- -->

- At least 2–3 times per semester (formal meetings).

- Informal mentoring available anytime (open-door policy).

<!-- -->

- **Mentoring Record:** Minutes of meetings, attendance sheets, action taken reports maintained.

## 3. Implementation

- Mentoring Cell/Coordinator assigns students to faculty mentors.

- Mentoring schedules (monthly/semester-wise) published by departments.

- Both physical and online mentoring modes used (ERP, LMS, email, video calls).

- Final year students often serve as peer mentors for juniors in academics and projects.

## 4. Monitoring & Reporting

- **Mentor Diaries/ Logbooks:** Track discussions, issues raised, and follow-ups.

- **Department-Level Review:** HoD/IQAC verifies mentoring records.

- **Institution-Level Review:** Annual reports on mentoring effectiveness submitted to Governing Body.

- **Feedback System:** Students give feedback on mentor effectiveness.

## 5. Effectiveness & Impact Studies

- **Academic Improvement:** Better performance of weak learners, reduced dropout rates.

- **Professional Outcomes:** Increased placement/higher studies success.

- **Wellness Outcomes:** Improved student satisfaction, stress management.

- **Peer Mentoring Impact:** Juniors adapt faster with help from final-year seniors.

# 7. Mentoring schedules and frequency records

Definition

- Mentoring schedules = the planned calendar of mentor–mentee meetings for each semester/year.

- Frequency records = the logged actuals of meetings held, with proof (minutes, attendance, action taken), and variance vs plan.

Minimum standards to target (for strong scoring)

- Faculty: Student ratio: 1:15–1:20 (program-wise allocation published).

- Frequency: ≥2–3 formal meetings per semester (early, mid, pre-exam) + on-demand 1:1s. 1:1s.

- **Coverage:** ≥95% students mapped to mentors with visible allocation order/ERP list.

- **Attendance:** median 275–80% across meetings.

## What to pre-publish at term start (Schedule)

- **Department schedule:** month-wise meeting windows (e.g., Weeks 2, 7, 12).

- **Mentor-wise plan:** planned count per semester, planned dates, mode (physical/online/hybrid).

- **Scope per meeting:** academics/labs → careers/higher studies → exam readiness/wellness.

- **Communication evidence:** circular/ERP notice/email to mentees; keep PDF/URL.

## What to record after each meeting (Frequency records)

- Date & mode, attendance %, agenda covered (course/lab/career/wellness).

- Issues logged (academic backlogs, lab constraints, internships, personal).

- Actions & referrals (remedials, counseling, T&P, COE, ICC/GRC) with owners & due dates.

- Evidence links (signed minutes, attendance sheet, photos/screenshot, ERP log).

## How to measure compliance (simple metrics)

- **Planned vs Actual:** Compliance = Actual meetings/ Planned meetings (target ≥100%).

- **Shortfall flag:** “Shortfall: (Planned-Actual)” if Actual \< Planned.

- **Timeliness:** % meetings held within planned window (±7 days).

- **Effectiveness:** issue closure rate within SLA (e.g., 80% within 14 days).

## Suggested semester template (example)

- **M1 (Weeks 1–2):** Orientation, course plan, slow-learner identification, internship interest capture.

- **M2 (Weeks 6–7):** Lab/project progress, GPA risk mitigation, resume/aptitude prep.

- **M3 (Weeks 12–13):** Pre-exam support, backpaper plan, career/HS action plan, carryforward issues.

Modes & inclusivity

- Physical (preferred for first/last meets), Online (accessibility/placements), Hybrid (hostellers/day scholars).

- Ensure accessible slots (no timetable clashes); alternate slots for absentees; record make-up sessions.

Peer mentoring (senior → junior)

- Assign final-year buddies per 8–10 juniors; sync with faculty mentor plan.

- Log touchpoints (orientation, mid-term, pre-exam) and topics (study skills, labs, campus systems).

Monitoring & governance

- **Weekly/Monthly HOD review:** compliance & shortfalls, reschedule actions.

- **IQAC quarterly review:** dashboards (dept-wise compliance, attendance, outcomes).

- **Annual consolidation:** trends (CAY, CAYm1, CAYm2), impact cases, included in Annual Report.

## Evidence pack (keep ready for visit)

- Allocation order/ERP mapping (mentor–mentee lists with ratio).

- Term schedule (department & mentor-level) + notices/emails.

- Signed minutes & attendance for each meet (PDFs), photos/screenshots, ERP extracts.

- Action-taken reports (ATR) and closure proofs (e.g., remedial timetables, counseling slips).

- Student feedback summary on mentoring utility (≥4/5 target).

Data structure to maintain (tracker fields)

- Mentor • Dept • Batch • Planned meetings (count/dates) • Actual meetings (autocounted) • Mode • Attendance % • Issues • Actions • Evidence links • Compliance status.

Privacy & escalation

- Avoid sensitive details in shared minutes; keep confidential notes separately.

- Route POSH/grievance items via ICC/GRC; log only ticket/reference IDs.

Common pitfalls (avoid)

- Plans without actuals/evidence; inflated entries; missing attendance or ATR.

- \>20 mentees per mentor without balancing; skipped make-ups for absentees.

- Unannounced schedule changes with no updated notice.

Quick KPI targets for SSR

- Schedule compliance ≥90%, attendance ≥75%, issue-closure ≥80% within SLA.

- Coverage 100% mentor mapping; documented impact (CGPA lift, internship/offer gains, reduced backlogs).

# 9. Impact studies (dropout reduction, placement improvement)

- Purpose

<!-- -->

- Demonstrate that mentoring, training, and governance produce measurable outcomes.

- Provide evidence-based comparisons showing dropout reduction and placement improvement across years/cohorts.

<!-- -->

- Define clear outcomes

<!-- -->

- **Dropout reduction:** decrease in students who discontinue a program before completion.

- **Placement improvement:** increase in % students placed, quality of offers (CTC, role), and time-to-offer.

<!-- -->

- Key metrics & formulas

<!-- -->

- Annual Dropout Rate (%) = (Number of students who discontinued during year ÷ Total enrolled at year start) × 100.

- Retention Rate (%) = 100 – Annual Dropout Rate.

- Cohort Completion Rate (%) = (Graduated in T years ÷ Cohort admitted in Year 0) × 100.

- Placement Rate (%) = (Number of students with final offers ÷ Number of eligible students) × 100.

- Median CTC (₹) and IQR (quality dispersion).

- Offer Speed (days) = Offer Date – Placement Window Start.

- Uplift (pp) = Placement Rate (After) – Placement Rate (Before) in percentage points.

- At-risk coverage (%) = (At-risk students mentored ÷ Total at-risk) × 100.

<!-- -->

- Study design (before–after & cohorting)

# 11. Meeting attendance sheets and reports

## Purpose

- Prove that mentoring meetings actually occurred, who attended, and what was covered.

- Enable audit of planned vs. conducted sessions, attendance levels, and actions taken.

## What an attendance sheet must capture (minimum fields)

- Unique Meeting ID (e.g., CSE-S1-M2), Date, Start–End Time, Venue/Mode (Physical/Online/Hybrid).

- Mentor name & department, Program/Batch, Topic/Agenda.

- Student ID & Name for every mentee mapped to the mentor.

- **Attendance status per mentee:** Present/ Late / Excused / Absent.

- **Signatures/evidence:** physical sign sheet or online log (screenshot, platform attendance, recording link).

- Evidence link to minutes, photos, ERP/LMS extract.

Status coding that works well

- Present (P) = attended fully; Late (L) = joined late but attended; Excused (E) = prior approved absence; Absent (A) = no show.

- For online sessions, accept platform logs as presence evidence; for hybrid, retain both sign sheet and platform report.

Good practices for the raw sheet

- Maintain a sorted roster (by roll/ID) to avoid duplicates and omissions.

- Use drop-downs for status to avoid free-text errors.

- Capture time-in/time-out when feasible (strengthens audit readiness).

- Use a consistent Meeting ID across attendance, minutes, photos, and reports.

## What a meeting report must contain (1–2 pages)

- Meeting metadata (ID, Date, Mentor, Batch, Mode, Agenda).

- **Attendance summary:** planned headcount; counts of P/L/E/A; effective attendance % (P+L+E)/Planned.

- **Discussion summary:** key issues (academics, labs, career, wellness) and decisions.

- **Action Taken Report (ATR):** item, owner (mentor/student/office), due date, status.

- **Attachments list:** minutes PDF, sign sheet or platform export, photos/screenshots, ERP links.

Computation & KPIs (simple, NBA-friendly)

- Effective Attendance % = ((Present + Late + Excused) ÷ Planned) × 100.

- Compliance to plan = Actual meetings ÷ Planned meetings (flag shortfall if \<1.0).

- Issue closure rate = Closed ATR items ÷ Total ATR items (target ≥80% within 14 days).

Frequency records linkage

- Map each meeting to the semester plan (e.g., early/mid/end) and record actual dates.

- If a planned window is missed, record make-up date and reason for slippage.

## Evidence standards (physical & online)

- **Physical:** signed attendance sheet, dated photos with visible class/board, scanned PDF.

- **Online:** platform participant report (Meet/Teams/Zoom), chat export, screenshot with date/time.

- **Hybrid:** combine both; keep one consolidated meeting report.

Privacy & compliance

- Do not record sensitive personal details in shared minutes; keep confidential notes separately.

- Route harassment/grievance matters via ICC/GRC; reference only ticket IDs in reports.

Archiving & naming

- **Folder per semester:** Mentoring/2025-Sem1/\<Dept\>/\<Mentor\>/.

- **Files named consistently:** MeetingID_Attendance.xlsx, MeetingID_Minutes.pdf, MeetingID_Photos.zip.

- Maintain CAY, CAYm1, CAYm2 archives for NBA.

## How auditors typically verify

- Randomly pick Meeting IDs and ask for matching attendance, minutes, photos, and ERP logs.

- Cross-check headcount vs. mentor–mentee roster and dates vs. published schedule.

- Look for ATR follow-through in subsequent meetings.

Common pitfalls (and fixes)

- Missing signatures/logs → always attach at least one verifiable artifact.

- Free-text statuses → enforce drop-downs (P/L/E/A).

- Inconsistent Meeting IDs → predefine a coding scheme and include in circular.

- No link to actions → include a small ATR table in every report.

Using the provided Excel (quick mapping)

- Enter each participant under Attendance_Log with the same Meeting ID.

- In Meetings sheet, list each Meeting ID once; counts and Attendance % auto-calculate.

- Attach minutes/photos/ERP links in the Evidence columns for both sheets.

Targets to mention in SSR

- ≥2–3 meetings per semester per mentor; 275–80% median effective attendance.

- ≥90% compliance to planned schedule; ≥80% ATR closure within 14 days.

- Documented impact cases (grade uplift, lab completion, internship conversions).

# 18. Participation Statistics

## 1. What Are Participation Statistics?

- They represent the percentage of stakeholders (students, alumni, employers, faculty, parents) who actually gave feedback against the total eligible population.

- This ensures that feedback is representative and authentic, not based on a small or biased sample.

## 2. Stakeholder Categories & Formula

- **Students:**

> Participation % = No. of Students Responded/Total Students Enrolled\* 100

- **Alumni:**

> Participation % = No. of Alumni Responded/Total Alumni Contacted\* 100

- **Employers:**

> Participation % = No. of Employers Responded/Total Employers Contacted\* 100

- **Faculty (if applicable):**

> Participation % = No. of Faculty Responded/Total Faculty\* 100

- **Parents (optional):** Same formula as above.

- **Students:** 70–90% response rate is considered strong.

- **Alumni:** 20–40% is acceptable (since outreach is more difficult).

# 42. Transparency

- Grievance redressal policies (students & faculty).

> 2\. Delegation of Financial Powers

> 6\. Stakeholder Awareness

- **Students:** aware of academic rules, grievance systems, placement processes.

# 47. Variance Reports (Budget vs Actual Expenditure)

## 1. Purpose of Variance Reports

- NBA requires not just allocation & utilization records but also analysis of deviations between Budgeted vs Actual Expenditure.

- Proves that the institute monitors financial discipline, investigates underspending/overspending, and takes corrective actions.

- Strengthens transparency, accountability, and continuous improvement.

## 2. Essential Components of a Variance Report

**For each head of expenditure (institute/program-level):**

- Budgeted Amount (INR Lakhs) – approved by BoG/GB/GC.

- Actual Expenditure (INR Lakhs) –as per audited accounts.

- Variance (INR Lakhs) – Actual – Budgeted.

- Variance % – (Variance + Budgeted) × 100.

- Reasons for Variance – explanation for underspending or overspending.

- Corrective Action Taken – adjustments made for future budgets.

- Variance Reports must be prepared year-wise (CFYm3, CFYm2, CFYm1, CFY).

- NBA expects institutions to align policies, practices, and projects with UN-SDGs (2030 Agenda).

- Ensures that students and faculty contribute to sustainability, inclusivity, and responsible innovation.

- Focus areas include green energy, waste reduction, water preservation, net-zero commitments, and quality education.

## 2. Key Areas of Implementation & Examples

## A. Green Energy (SDG 7 – Affordable & Clean Energy)

- Installation of solar panels, wind power units, and energy-efficient equipment.

- Smart classroom lighting with motion sensors, LED bulbs.

- **Evidence:** Solar energy generation records, energy audit reports, photos of installations.

## B. Waste Management (SDG 12 – Responsible Consumption & Production)

- Segregation of biodegradable, plastic, and e-waste.

- Partnerships with recyclers for e-waste disposal.

- Composting units for organic waste.

- **Evidence:** Waste audit reports, MoUs with recyclers, photos of composting sites.

## C. Water Conservation (SDG 6 – Clean Water & Sanitation)

- Rainwater harvesting units installed on campus.

- Water recycling plants for greywater usage in gardening.

- Regular water audits and awareness campaigns.

- **Evidence:** Water audit reports, rainwater harvesting structures, lab tests of water quality.

## D. Net Zero Commitment (SDG 13 – Climate Action)

- Roadmap to achieve carbon neutrality by reducing campus emissions.

- Tree plantation drives and green campus initiatives.

- Reduced carbon footprint through e-mobility (bicycles, EV charging stations).

- **Evidence:** Carbon audit reports, plantation data, transport policy.

## E. Quality Education (SDG 4 – Inclusive & Equitable Education)

- Free access to MOOCs, NPTEL, SWAYAM, DSpace repositories.

- Skill development centers for employability and entrepreneurship.

- **Evidence:** LMS screenshots, course enrollment data, student project reports.

## F. Reuse, Recycle & Reduce Practices (SDG 12 – Responsible Consumption)

- Paperless governance through ERP, e-governance, digital library.

- Use of recycled water, reused furniture/lab equipment.

- Banning of single-use plastics on campus.

- **Evidence:** ERP screenshots, digital workflows, plastic-free policy documents.

## 3. Implementation Strategies

- **Academic Projects:**

- Student capstone projects on renewable energy, waste management, water conservation.

- Research papers on sustainable technologies.

<!-- -->

- **R&D Activities:**

<!-- -->

- Funded research on solar, bioenergy, water purification, sustainable agriculture.

- Patents in renewable technologies.

<!-- -->

- **Entrepreneurial Activities:**

<!-- -->

- Incubation support for startups in green energy, recycling, eco-products.

- Student ventures promoting sustainability.

<!-- -->

- **Outreach & Extension Programs:**

<!-- -->

- Awareness campaigns in nearby communities on water conservation, plastic reduction, renewable adoption.

- Collaboration with NGOs, local governments under Unnat Bharat Abhiyan.

## 4. Documentation & Evidence

- Policy documents on energy, waste, and water management.

- **Audit reports:** Energy audit, water audit, waste audit, carbon footprint audit.

- MoUs with sustainability partners (NGOs, recyclers, renewable companies).

- Reports & photos of student/faculty projects, workshops, plantation drives.

- **R&D Evidence:** Publications, patents, prototypes.

- **Entrepreneurial Evidence:** Startup details, incubation records, funding reports.

## 5. Impact Indicators

- Reduction in electricity bills due to renewable adoption.

- Increase in water savings (liters per year) via rainwater harvesting.

- Reduced waste sent to landfill through recycling initiatives.

- Higher student awareness measured via surveys and NSS/NCC participation.

- Enhanced community development through outreach projects.

## 6. NBA Expectation

- Clear linkage of SDGs with institutional practices.

- Evidence of sustained initiatives for at least 3 years (CAY, CAYm1, CAYm2).

- Impact reports showing measurable outcomes (energy saved, water recycled, waste reduced, students benefitted).

- Integration of SDG initiatives into curriculum, research, outreach, and governance.

# 64. Faculty Performance Appraisal and Development System (FPADS)

## 1. Purpose of FPADS

- **Faculty in Higher Education Institutions (HEIs) perform multi-dimensional roles:** teaching, research, innovation, administration, and community service.

- **An effective FPADS ensures:**

<!-- -->

- Recognition and motivation for high-performing faculty.

- Identification of faculty requiring support and training.

- Alignment of faculty contributions with institutional vision and NBA accreditation parameters.

- Continuous professional growth and academic excellence.

## 2. Components of Faculty Performance Appraisal

## A. Teaching & Instructional Role

- Quality of course delivery, innovation in pedagogy, ICT integration.

- Student feedback scores on teaching-learning effectiveness.

- Development of course materials, lab manuals, online resources.

## B. Research & Innovation

- Research publications in Scopus/SCl-indexed journals.

- Funded projects, consultancy assignments, patents, prototypes.

- Participation in FDPs, workshops, seminars for continuous knowledge renewal.

## C. Industry & Community Engagement

- Industry-linked projects, internships guided, MoUs facilitated.

- Consultancy services provided to local industries.

- Outreach activities, community service (NSS/NCC/UBA guidance).

## D. Administrative & Institutional Responsibilities

- Contributions as HOD, Dean, IQAC Coordinator, NBA/NAAC coordinator, etc.

- Roles in examination cell, placement cell, cultural/technical events.

- Participation in institutional committees and decision-making.

## E. Professional Development

- Participation in FDPs, MOOCs, refresher/orientation courses.

- Membership in professional bodies (IEEE, ISTE, CSI, IETE, etc.).

- Achievements, recognitions, awards at state/national/international level.

## 3. Appraisal Mechanism

- **Self-Appraisal:** Faculty submit self-appraisal forms highlighting achievements.

- **Student Feedback:** Consolidated analysis of student evaluations.

- **Peer Review:** Assessment by senior colleagues/HoDs on teaching & institutional contributions.

- **HoD/Principal Review:** Final appraisal including research, community service, and administrative contributions.

- **Weightage Distribution:**

<!-- -->

- **Teaching:** 40–50%

- **Research & Publications:** 20–25%

- **Industry/Community Service:** 10–15%

- **Administration:** 10–15%

- **Professional Development:** 5–10%

## 4. Faculty Development System (FDS)

- Faculty with excellent appraisal scores are recognized with awards, promotions, and incentives.

- **Faculty with low appraisal scores receive:**

<!-- -->

- Mentoring by senior faculty.

- Mandatory participation in pedagogy/ICT training programs.

- Opportunities for skill upgradation (AI, Data Science, Emerging Tech).

<!-- -->

- Institute organizes Faculty Development Programs (FDPs) to upgrade competencies.

- Sabbatical/industry immersion for practical exposure.

## 5. Documentation for NBA

- Faculty Appraisal Policy Document (clearly defining criteria & weightages).

- Self-Appraisal Forms (for CAY, CAYm1, CAYm2).

- Consolidated Appraisal Reports (department-wise, year-wise).

- **Evidence of Implementation:**

<!-- -->

- Student feedback reports.

- Faculty research/publication records.

- FDP participation certificates.

- Industry consultancy/project reports.

<!-- -->

- **Impact Evidence:** Improvement in faculty research output, teaching quality, student satisfaction, and institutional rankings.

## 6. Example (FPADS in Practice)

- **Issue Identified:** Low student feedback for a course in Digital Electronics.

- **Action Taken:** Faculty guided to attend FDP on Innovative Teaching Practices.

- **Evidence:** FDP participation certificate, revised course plan.

- **Impact:** Student feedback improved from 3.5/5 → 4.2/5 in next cycle.

## 7. NBA Expectation

- A structured FPADS policy with clear criteria, well-implemented for at least 3 years (CAY, CAYm1, CAYm2).

- Evidence that appraisal leads to faculty growth, motivation, and institutional development.

- Proof that underperforming faculty are supported, not penalized.

# 75. Outreach Activities are not symbolic but result-oriented

## 1. Moving Beyond Symbolism

- Symbolic outreach = one-day events with photos but no measurable impact.

- NBA expects evidence that activities produce real, sustained outcomes.

## 2. Indicators of Result-Oriented Outreach

- **Clear Objectives:** Each outreach activity should have a stated goal (e.g., *train 200 villagers in digital payments, plant 500 trees and ensure 80% survival*).

- **Measurable Outcomes:** Show quantifiable results, not just participation numbers.

<!-- -->

- No. of beneficiaries reached (patients treated, villagers trained, families supported).

- Resources mobilized (blood units collected, saplings planted, awareness kits distributed).

<!-- -->

- **Impact Evidence:**

<!-- -->

- Beneficiary feedback (community surveys, testimonials).

- Before-and-after comparisons (e.g., sanitation levels, digital adoption rates).

- Long-term benefits (villages adopting solar lamps, sustained waste segregation practices).

## 3. Documentation Practices

- Impact Assessment Reports (activity-wise → outcomes achieved, SDG alignment, evidence attached).

- Student Reflection & Testimonial Records (skills gained, empathy developed, leadership experience).

- Community/Beneficiary Feedback Forms (acknowledgement letters from Panchayats, NGOs, schools).

- **Follow-up Actions:** Evidence of repeated engagement, not one-time activity (e.g., *annual health camp, continued digital literacy workshops*).

## 4. Evidence for NBA Peer Team

- Outreach Tracker → Year, Date, Venue, Beneficiaries, Outcomes, Evidence Link.

- Photographs and media coverage showing community engagement.

- Certificates issued to students & acknowledgments from partners (NGOs, Panchayats).

- MoUs proving long-term collaborations.

- **Summary charts → Year-wise outreach:** *Activities conducted, Students involved, Beneficiaries reached, Outcomes achieved*.

## 5. Example

- **Symbolic:** “Tree plantation drive conducted, 500 saplings planted.”

- **Result-Oriented:** “Tree plantation drive conducted, 500 saplings planted, survival rate monitored at 80% after 6 months. Community Eco-Club formed for maintenance.”
